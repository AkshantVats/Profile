Day 89 — Five Years of Infra Interviews — What “Staff” Signals Look Like

Repo count is legible on a resume. A platform narrative is what's legible to an interview panel — and 89 days of shipped tickets doesn't produce one automatically.

AS
Akshant Sharma
Experience · Day 89 of 150
August 11, 2026
7 min read

Today's code work is a product essay: docs/hn-launch-routeiq.md compresses five modules — a semantic cache, a budget enforcer, a fingerprint layer, a quality scorer, a fallback router — shipped across three months of separate day-tickets into one Show HN post. Writing it surfaced a question that outlasts RouteIQ specifically: an infra interview panel is asking for the exact same compression, live, with no essay to lean on.

Five years of sitting on both sides of infra interview loops — as a candidate walking a panel through a system, and later as an interviewer listening for the same thing in someone else's answer — points at one consistent tell. It isn't depth on a single system.

Plenty of Senior-level answers have that depth. It's whether the depth connects to anything.

The Tell Isn't Depth, It's Connection

A strong Senior-level answer to "tell me about a system you built" is usually excellent in isolation: clear problem statement, the tradeoffs that were actually weighed, honest scope about what was personally built versus what a team already had running. That answer can be completely correct and still read as one good story pulled out of a much longer list of tickets.

The answer that reads differently is the one where the candidate, without being asked, connects that system to the one they described two questions earlier — not because the panel steered them there, but because the two systems actually share a design decision, and the candidate had already noticed.

So what: a panel isn't grading whether one system was built well; they're checking whether the candidate's mental model of their own work is a list or a graph — and a graph is what "operates at the next level already" actually looks like from the outside.

Scope — pattern observed across roles, not a single-company claim
This post reflects on interview patterns across roughly five years and several infra-focused roles (per the resume: Walmart Labs, Delivery Hero, Agoda, Wayfair), not one specific company's loop or one specific interviewer's rubric. Where it draws on scope or tenure, it stays inside what's documented in this build's own resume context file — no invented interview transcripts, no claims about a particular employer's exact bar.

Repo Count Is a Resume Metric, Not an Interview One

It's tempting to treat a body of work's legibility as a counting problem: how many repos, how many PRs, how many systems touched. That's the metric a resume optimizes for, because a resume is skimmed in seconds and a bullet count is fast to parse.

An interview panel has forty-five minutes and isn't counting. What they're actually testing is whether a candidate can take that same bullet list and produce the argument for why it's one body of work with a point of view, not five unrelated line items that happen to share a GitHub org.

What gets shownWhat a resume optimizes forWhat an interview panel is actually listening for
Five modules shipped over three monthsFive bullet points, five repo linksOne sentence explaining why they're the same product
A budget enforcer and a routing proxyTwo separate "built X" linesWhether the candidate notices they compose as one control plane
A design decision made on day 65Not mentioned againWhether it's still load-bearing in the day 89 answer

Nothing about the underlying work changes between those two columns. What changes is whether the connective tissue between tickets was ever made explicit, out loud, by the person who did the work — and a resume bullet has no room to do that, so the interview is the first and only place it can happen.

So what: optimizing a resume for repo count and optimizing an interview answer for narrative are two different skills, and only one of them is exercised by the act of shipping the work itself.

Writing the Essay Is the Rehearsal

Today's docs/hn-launch-routeiq.md forced exactly this compression on paper, ahead of any interview: five modules, one control-plane framing, four numbered design decisions that hold across all five, and an honest-limitations section that doesn't pretend the story is cleaner than it is. That's not a coincidence of format — a Show HN post and a Staff-level interview answer are solving the identical compression problem for two different five-minute audiences.

The essay is the lower-stakes rehearsal: it can be redrafted, fact-checked against docs/context/resume-extracted.md-equivalent source material, and reviewed before anyone reads it. The interview answer is the same argument, live, with no second draft. Writing the essay first is what makes the live version fluent instead of improvised.

So what: practicing the platform narrative in writing, where it can be checked and corrected, is what makes it available under interview pressure — waiting to construct it live is asking working memory to do drafting work it isn't built for.

Five tickets don't become one story by being shipped in the same repo. They become one story the first time someone says out loud why they're the same decision made five times.

What Doesn't Transfer From the Written Version

The essay format has one advantage an interview answer never gets: nobody interrupts it mid-paragraph to ask why decision three didn't apply to module four. A live answer has to survive that question, and the honest response is sometimes "it didn't, and here's why that's fine" rather than a tidier story where every decision generalizes perfectly.

docs/hn-launch-routeiq.md's own honest-limitations section is the closest thing to rehearsing that answer in writing: naming what a design choice didn't cover, instead of letting the reader assume total coverage. An interview version of that same instinct is the difference between a candidate who oversells a pattern's generality under a pointed follow-up and one who already knows, and says, where it stops.

So what: the platform narrative that reads as Staff-level isn't the one with no gaps — it's the one where the gaps were already found and named before anyone in the room had to find them first.
