Day 30 — Step 7 Failed Silently — And Nobody Had a Span

How a silent Route Consumer deduplication failure cost two hours of diagnosis — and shaped TraceForge’s span schema

AS
Akshant Sharma
Experience · Day 30 of 150
July 3, 2026
16 min read

The dashboard showed green on every metric I had. The Order SQS queues were near zero, the OSRM cluster was healthy, and every Route Service pod was processing normally. Between Step 6 — order assignment — and Step 8 — route confirmation — Step 7 was silently dropping navigation updates for one specific order state transition, and nobody knew for twenty minutes until a support ticket arrived: rider at the wrong drop-off, Step 8 reporting success, Step 7 having never called OSRM.

That incident is what I think about whenever someone tells me their distributed system is fully monitored. Full monitoring, in most organizations I have seen, means visible at service boundaries — queue depth, consumer lag, end-to-end latency. Not inside services, between the moment a message is ACK’d and the moment the downstream write either fires or doesn’t.

The gap between “message consumed” and “side effect produced” is the space where Step 7 lived and died. Without a span covering that transition, reconstructing what happened required correlating eight log streams across four services — two hours of manual work that a single span containing the deduplication decision would have resolved in thirty seconds.

The Async Pipeline: What “Monitored” Actually Meant

Delivery Hero’s Global Logistics Platform ran more than 1M daily orders on EKS, with 5k+ real-time OSRM route updates per second. Synchronous request chains were impractical at that throughput, so the pipeline was deliberately async: Order SQS consumed lifecycle events — PLACED, PICKED UP, RIDER ENQUE, RIDER PICKED UP — Route Consumers read from those queues and dispatched to the OSRM cluster, and the Route object was written back asynchronously. The design was intentional and the right trade-off; the observability gap was not.

“Fully monitored” meant queue depth dashboards on Order SQS, consumer group lag on Route Consumer pods, OSRM cluster CPU and memory, EKS node health, and end-to-end latency at the order level. What we did not have was per-step spans tracing which consumer processed which event, whether OSRM dispatch was called for a specific event, or the intermediate state between message consumption and the downstream Route object write. Monitoring was designed around pipeline boundaries, not pipeline internals.

The gap was invisible because it appeared to work. Queue depth stayed low — messages were consumed. Consumer lag was acceptable. Route objects updated correctly for 99.97% of orders. The 0.03% where Step 7 silently dropped had no dashboard representation, no alert, no counter — just a support ticket that arrived twenty minutes later when a rider followed the wrong route.

Non-software analogy — warehouse staging
A warehouse that monitors when forklifts pick up pallets and when trucks depart, but not what happens in the staging area between. If a pallet gets mis-staged, the “pick up” event fires, the “truck departed” event fires, and the staging area failure is invisible. The audit trail has two events. The failure happened between them.

“Fully monitored” means visible at service boundaries. The interesting failures happen inside services, between the event consumption ACK and the downstream effect — queue-level monitoring cannot see them.

Step 7: The Invisible Transition

Step 7 was the rider navigation update: when an order transitioned to RIDER PICKED UP, the Route Service recalculated the delivery route from the rider’s current GPS location to the customer’s drop-off, updated the OSRM routing database, and wrote a new Route object. The operation was non-idempotent — calling OSRM twice with the same pickup confirmation produced different routes because the rider had moved between calls.

The failure was a race condition in the Route Consumer’s OSRM dispatch logic. Two events for the same order arrived 340ms apart under surge conditions with 10k+ concurrent requests, and the in-memory deduplication cache held a stale negative entry from the first event’s processing. The second RIDER PICKED UP event matched the deduplication key, was treated as a duplicate, and was dropped before OSRM dispatch. The SQS message was ACK’d and the consumer moved on — no error logged, because the deduplication logic used a silent early return by design.

The only evidence of the failure was the absence of a Route object update timestamp. The Route object existed and had a timestamp from Step 3 — the initial route calculation at order placement — but not from Step 7 — the pickup update. The customer’s app showed a route to the restaurant, not their drop-off address, because Step 7 had never updated it.

Non-software analogy — postal deduplication failure
A postal worker whose “already delivered” stamp pad has ink from the wrong address stamps a package delivered based on street name matching — not full address — and moves on. Package sits in depot. Recipient gets a “delivered” notification. The step between “stamped as processed” and “physically delivered” was never traced.

Silent deduplication logic that returns early without a log entry is a correctness hazard — every early-return branch that produces no side effect needs a trace span or it becomes an invisible failure mode.

Diagnosing Without Spans: The Two-Hour Tax

The diagnosis started from the support ticket. Eight log streams needed to be correlated: Order Service logs, Route Service logs, Route Consumer logs from two separate pods, OSRM access logs, the SQS dead-letter queue, EKS pod events, and the Route object audit history. None of them said “Step 7 did not run for order X.” The evidence was the absence of evidence — which is much harder to find than a logged error.

The reconstruction took two hours. I found two RIDER PICKED UP events 340ms apart with identical content. The Route Consumer log showed a cache hit on the second event but with no record of what the cache hit caused, because the early return was silent. The Route object’s last-modified timestamp was 47 minutes before the RIDER PICKED UP events, inconsistent with a successful Step 7 execution. The OSRM access log showed no route recalculation for that order after RIDER PICKED UP. Each piece of evidence lived in a different system, queried separately, correlated manually by order ID across clocks that weren’t synchronized.

A single span for the deduplication decision would have contained the order ID, event type, cache key, cache hit result, and branch taken. Thirty seconds to query. The two-hour diagnosis was a direct consequence of instrumentation-free deduplication logic — not because the failure was complex, but because it happened in a space that produced no observable signal.

Non-software analogy — accident reconstruction
Diagnosing without spans is like reconstructing a car accident from witness statements, traffic camera footage, tire marks, and weather reports — each from a different source, each with different timestamps, each requiring you to decide which to trust. A dashcam recording of the car’s perspective answers the question in ten seconds. Distributed tracing is the dashcam. Log correlation across eight systems is the accident reconstruction.

The two hours spent diagnosing a silent async failure is the exact measure of what a single well-placed span would have saved — the tax you pay once per incident, but only on the incident where you can least afford it.

What the Fix Required: Making Silent Branches Visible

The fix had two parts. First, the deduplication logic’s early-return branch got an explicit log entry: order ID, event type, cache key, reason for early return, and a monotonic counter of how often this branch had fired in the last 60 seconds. A silent decision became a visible one.

Second, a downstream metric was added — the counter route_consumer_dedup_hit_total with labels for event type and order state. The dashboard could now alert if deduplication hit rates exceeded expected baselines, making an anomaly visible as a deviation from expected behaviour rather than invisible until a customer escalated.

Neither fix prevented recurrence if the same race condition hit again. They made the failure immediately visible as a deviation from expected behaviour, rather than invisible until a customer escalated. The deeper fix — correcting the deduplication key to include a monotonic sequence number rather than a content hash — came the following sprint, after observability made the actual failure mode diagnosable. Observability came first because without it, diagnosis was impossible and the correct fix was unknowable.

Non-software analogy — vending machine receipt
The log entry and metric are like adding a receipt printer to a vending machine that previously dispensed silently. The machine still makes the same dispensing decision — valid selection gets a snack, invalid gets nothing. But now there’s a printed record of every decision, including every “unavailable” response. The receipt doesn’t fix a broken dispensing mechanism. It makes broken dispensing visible rather than invisible, which is the prerequisite for fixing it.

Making a silent branch visible — through a log entry, a metric, or a span — is not the same as fixing the underlying logic, but it is the prerequisite for being able to diagnose and fix it when it fails in production.

An AI agent’s execution is structurally identical to the Delivery Hero rider tracking pipeline: a sequence of async steps, each producing a side effect the next step depends on. A ReAct loop calling tools in sequence — Read, Bash, Edit, Bash again — has exactly the same failure mode: Step 7 can fail silently, and without a span it is invisible until the downstream effect is wrong. In the DH pipeline, “downstream effect” means a rider at the wrong address; in an AI agent, it means a code change that was never written to disk.

agent-trace-collector is built to prevent the specific failure mode I spent two hours diagnosing in Berlin. Every tool call in an agent run gets a span: tool_name, tool_kind, status, latency_ms. Every silent early-return — a tool that returns without producing its expected side effect — gets captured as a span with status ERROR and an error_message. The execution tree reconstructed from the flat span stream shows exactly what the agent did, step by step, and where it stopped producing effects — one trace query, not eight log streams.

The design decision to store parent_span_id is the direct consequence of the DH diagnosis: to find a silent failure in an async pipeline, you need to know what was supposed to happen next and didn’t. parent_span_id establishes the expected execution tree. A missing child span where a child should exist — a sub_agent call with no child model_call — is a visible gap, exactly what the DH pipeline lacked.

Non-software analogy — dashcam
agent-trace-collector is the dashcam installed after the accident. Not because we expect the accident to recur immediately, but because we know the road well enough now to understand where the next blind spot is. The DH incident taught me exactly which step doesn’t get a span by default — the async dispatch inside a consumer that logs nothing when it takes an early exit. The dashcam records every turn, including the silent ones.

The DH incident’s diagnosis cost was a direct function of missing spans; agent-trace-collector encodes the lesson as a schema where every step produces a span — including the silent ones.

What I’d Do Differently

I would instrument every branch of a deduplication decision, not just the success path. The DH deduplication logic emitted metrics for messages processed, not messages rejected. A deduplication cache that silently drops events is the most dangerous kind — it makes a correctness decision while appearing to be a performance optimization. Every such decision belongs in a trace span.

I would treat async pipeline steps as first-class observability citizens. At DH, pipeline observability was designed around pipeline boundaries — queue depth, consumer lag, end-to-end latency. Steps inside pipelines were implementation details, opaque to monitoring. That trade-off made sense when trace collection was expensive; it was the wrong trade-off when a step’s silent failure had customer-visible consequences and required two hours to diagnose.

The principle that changed after that incident: the cost of a trace span is measured at write time, and the cost of not having one is measured at diagnosis time. For any step whose failure has customer impact — any step in the critical path between an async event and a customer-visible effect — the diagnosis cost of not having a span is always higher than the write cost of having one.

Non-software analogy — aircraft black box
Not instrumenting the critical path of an async pipeline is like flying with altitude and airspeed indicators but no black box. You know where the plane is going. You know how fast. If it crashes, you’ll reconstruct from radar traces, ground witnesses, and wreckage distribution. A black box doesn’t prevent the crash. It makes the investigation definitive rather than probabilistic. agent-trace-collector is the black box for agent runs.

The DH incident changed my instrument-on-the-critical-path rule from “add spans when debugging” to “add spans before the first production deployment” — because the first production incident is always the worst time to discover a step has no span.
