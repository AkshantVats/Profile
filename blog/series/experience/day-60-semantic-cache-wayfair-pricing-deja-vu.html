Day 60 — Semantic Cache — Wayfair Pricing Deja Vu

Aletheia's price cache only ever answered one question — is this the exact same key I already have — and Wayfair's near-real-time supplier feed kept asking it a question with no exact answer: is this update close enough to not matter?

A cache answers exactly one question: have I seen this key before?

Aletheia, Wayfair's retail price cache and GraphQL API in front of the storefront, answered that question about 20,000 times a second at peak — a SKU's catalog/ocid key in, a price out, no computation repeated for a key it had already served. That design is fast and correct for exactly one failure mode: a byte-for-byte identical key. It has nothing to say about a key that's almost the one it already has, which turned out to be the harder and more common case in a supplier feed nobody controlled.

What Aletheia Actually Cached

Delphi, Wayfair's retail price calculator, computed a price and pushed it downstream to Aletheia. Aletheia stored it in Bigtable and in-memory, served it to Storefront and Basket Service on read, and on any change republished a price-change event onto PriceChangeKafka — the sku_pricing_refresh topic specifically, which fanned out to search indexing and anything else that needed to know a price had moved.

Team-owned — Price Output Systems (Aletheia) and Pricing Calculators (Delphi)
Aletheia and Delphi were owned by their own teams at Wayfair, not by me. I'm describing the cache-invalidation contract those systems exposed to the rest of pricing, which is what my own work had to integrate against.

That contract works cleanly when a price genuinely changes. It says nothing about what to do when the input to a price changes by an amount too small to move the output — a supplier re-submitting a cost that's off by a rounding error, or the same cost resubmitted with a new timestamp because a batch job re-ran. Exact-key caching can't tell "this is new information" from "this is the same information wearing a different hash," because it was never designed to compare two keys — only to check one against itself.

So what: an exact-match cache is only ever answering "have I seen this exact key," and any question that starts with "is this basically the same as" has to be solved somewhere else, or it doesn't get solved at all.

The Near-Duplicate Problem, at 250k Updates a Supplier

The scale made the exact-match gap expensive rather than theoretical. Suppliers pushed 250,000-plus SKU updates each in near real-time, and I built the high-throughput bulk processing framework that absorbed that volume — distributed rate limiting and circuit breakers so one supplier's burst couldn't degrade the rest, running at 99.99% availability. Every one of those updates was a new key by definition, whether the underlying cost had actually moved or not.

My scope — Wayfair, Bengaluru, Nov 2024–Mar 2026, Sr. Software Engineer III, Led PAS & Pricing Promotion teams
I architected the GCP event-driven Global Pricing & Promotion Engine that cut price propagation from hours to sub-seconds across 20k+ suppliers, and built the bulk processing framework handling 250k+ SKU updates per supplier with distributed rate limiting and circuit breakers. This post describes a caching limitation that framework exposed, not a claim that semantic-cache-engine ran at Wayfair.

Treat every re-submission as a genuine price change and you invalidate Aletheia's cache and re-run Delphi's calculation for updates that changed nothing a customer would ever see — wasted compute on the calculation core, and a needless republish onto PriceChangeKafka that every downstream consumer has to process for no reason. Treat every re-submission as safe to ignore and you risk missing the one update in a thousand that's a real, customer-visible price drop wearing the same shape as the noise around it.

So what: at 250k updates per supplier, the choice isn't between "cache it" and "don't" — it's between wasting the calculation core on noise and risking a silent miss on the signal buried in that noise, and an exact-match key can't tell you which update is which.

Options for Building Tolerance

The honest options, ranked by what they actually buy you:

OptionWhat it catchesWhy it's not enough alone
TTL-only invalidationStale reads, eventuallyDoesn't distinguish noise from a real change within the window
Exact-hash dedupByte-identical resubmissionsMisses rounding drift and timestamp-only changes entirely
Fixed percentage-delta bandSmall price noise around one axis (cost)One threshold can't cover cost, MAP, and shipping deltas at once
Tenant-scoped similarity thresholdNear-duplicates across multiple correlated fieldsNeeds a false-positive budget and a decay policy — the harder build

Wayfair's pricing stack never had the fourth option; it lived with the middle two, tuned per feed by the teams that owned them. semantic-cache-engine's DESIGN.md picks the fourth option on purpose, because the input this time is a prompt, and a prompt has no single numeric field a percentage band could bound.

So what: a fixed tolerance band is the cheap version of "close enough," and it works until the thing you're comparing has more than one dimension that can drift independently — which is exactly where a single similarity score, tuned and monitored, earns its complexity.

What Transfers to Prompt Caching

semantic-cache-engine's DESIGN.md keys its similarity threshold per tenant, defaulting to 0.94 on embedding cosine similarity, with an explicit false-positive budget of 0.1% and a monotonic decay function that tightens the threshold as a cached entry ages toward its 30-day ceiling. That decay policy is the direct answer to a problem Wayfair's fixed-band approach never solved cleanly: a tolerance that's safe on day one of a cache entry's life is not automatically safe on day twenty-nine.

The rejected alternative worth naming: an ivfflat index over pgvector instead of hnsw. ivfflat is cheaper to build but requires a fixed number of clusters chosen up front, which fits a stable corpus poorly when tenant traffic patterns shift week to week — hnsw's graph structure tolerates that drift without a full reindex, at the cost of more memory per vector.

So what: Wayfair's pricing feed taught the tolerance-versus-precision tradeoff at the level of one numeric field; a prompt cache has to solve the same tradeoff across an entire embedding space, which is why the decay policy and the false-positive budget aren't optional extras — they're the part of the design that makes the threshold trustworthy past day one.

What This Design Doesn't Prove Yet

The honest gap: today is a DESIGN.md day, not a running system. There's no live embedding model wired up in this sandbox, no pgvector migration applied, and no measured false-positive rate against real prompt traffic — the 0.94 threshold and the 0.1% budget are design targets, not numbers pulled off a dashboard.

So what: a tolerance threshold is a hypothesis until it's measured against traffic that can actually violate it, and the honest version of this post says so instead of quoting 0.94 like it's already been proven safe.
