Day 146 — Cost/Latency/Quality Triangle — Executive Frame

Today's PR runs the same batch of prompts against a fine-tuned endpoint and a GPT-4o baseline and writes one CSV row per endpoint — cost, latency, and a quality proxy score. Every stakeholder conversation I've had about that table starts the same way regardless of employer: they ask for cheaper, faster, and better all at once, and the job is making the tradeoff visible instead of quietly picking two for them.

Today's harness in vllm-deploy-kit replays one batch of prompts against a fine-tuned endpoint and a GPT-4o baseline and writes a CSV: one row per endpoint, with a p50/p99 latency figure, a token-based cost estimate, and a quality proxy score. Nobody asked for exactly those three columns by accident. Cost, latency, and quality are the three things a business stakeholder asks about a model rollout, always in the same breath, usually followed by some version of "can we have all three."

The Contractor's Three Estimates

DS analogy — the contractor's three estimates
Ask a contractor to renovate a kitchen fast, cheap, and to a high standard, and a good one doesn't argue with the request — they hand back three different priced estimates instead. The rush estimate costs more because it means overtime crews and expedited material orders. The budget estimate takes longer because it waits for the contractor's normal-priced supplier delivery window, and the premium-materials estimate costs more than either regardless of timeline. The estimate itself is the tradeoff made visible; nobody has to take the contractor's word for which two they're actually getting.

So what: an executive rarely rejects a tradeoff once it's priced out on paper as separate options — what gets rejected is the version where an engineer just says "trust me" and picks silently on the stakeholder's behalf.

Team scope, stated plainly
At Wayfair I led the PAS team and the Pricing, Promotions & Discounts team at the same time, owning delivery across both product areas. Nothing about that role turned on one dramatic incident tied to a single cost/latency/quality call. It turned on the same three-way conversation recurring across two teams' worth of roadmap reviews, quarter after quarter, with a different number in each column every time.

Why the Table Beats the Verbal Promise

The design decision today's harness exists to inform is ordinary: keep sending production traffic to GPT-4o, or route a narrow task to a cheaper, self-hosted fine-tuned endpoint instead. A quality proxy score is an automated grade — often another model acting as a judge — standing in for a human reviewer's judgment, because a human can't read every request. That proxy is what makes the comparison affordable to run at all, and it's also the column most likely to be wrong in a way the other two aren't.

AxisGPT-4o baselineFine-tuned endpoint
Cost per requestExternal API pricing, no infra to runAmortized GPU cost, no per-call vendor fee
Latency (p50/p99)Network hop to a third-party APIIn-cluster, no external hop
Quality proxy scoreReference ceiling for the taskMust justify itself against that ceiling

The decision only makes sense once a threshold is named out loud: switch to the fine-tuned endpoint if its quality proxy score sits within an agreed margin of the baseline, given that cost and latency both already favor it. Two alternatives get rejected in the same breath — keep every request on GPT-4o regardless of cost, or chase full quality parity before shipping anything, which turns a load test into an open-ended research project.

So what: naming the threshold before looking at the numbers is what keeps the decision from becoming "whichever answer the loudest advocate in the room prefers" once the CSV is actually in front of people.

The consequence of picking the cheaper endpoint doesn't end at the decision meeting. Choosing it commits me to watching that quality proxy score over time, not just once, since a model that clears the bar today can drift below it as traffic shifts — the same reason this repo keeps a drift watcher on any endpoint it hands real traffic to.

So what: a cost/latency/quality decision made once from a single CSV isn't the end of the tradeoff, it's the start of an ongoing one that needs the same three columns checked again later.

The Number Executives Actually Read

In practice, cost is the column that gets read first, because a dollar figure needs no translation and latency and quality both do. That's a real risk: a stakeholder who anchors on the cheapest row before checking whether the quality proxy score actually held up is making the same mistake as a homeowner who books the rush estimate without confirming the contractor used the materials they quoted.

My job in that meeting isn't to hide the cost column — it's to make sure the quality row gets read with the same weight, in the same sitting, before a decision gets made off one number alone.

So what: a table with three honest columns can still produce a bad decision if only one of them gets read out loud, which means presenting the tradeoff well is as much a part of the job as measuring it correctly.

What I Still Don't Know

I don't know whether the quality proxy score today's harness produces is trustworthy enough to be the number that ends up on an executive's slide unqualified, since a proxy judge model has its own blind spots that a human reviewer wouldn't share. I also don't know whether collapsing three axes into a single "winner" recommendation loses information a stakeholder actually needed, versus keeping all three columns visible and letting them make the call themselves.

So what: the honest position is that today's CSV is good raw material for a conversation, not a verdict by itself — and conflating the two is exactly the failure mode this whole framing exists to avoid.
