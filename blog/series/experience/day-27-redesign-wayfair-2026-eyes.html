Day 27 — What I’d Redesign at Wayfair With 2026 Eyes

Ownership · leading two teams · pricing platform — three things I’d change now that I can see the full shape of what we built

There is a specific kind of clarity that comes from leaving a system. While you’re inside it, the constraints feel natural. You don’t question the ownership boundaries because the current ownership is how you know the system. You don’t question the data model because the current data model is what your code runs against. The design feels like physics rather than choice.

A few years after leaving Wayfair, I can see the pricing platform as a set of design choices rather than a set of facts. The Aletheia storefront cache serving ~20,590 requests per second. The PAS Core GraphQL endpoint handling ~3,414 average RPS with peaks to ~15,263. Delphi on c2d-standard-56 nodes — 56 vCPU and 224 GiB per node — running full-catalog pricing calculations. The five CloudSQL PostgreSQL databases spread across us-east4 for adjustments, corrections, FSP overrides, rate fencing, and main. These were engineering choices, and some of them I’d make differently today.

This post is about three of those choices. I’m not writing it to be critical of the engineers who made them — including myself. I’m writing it because the patterns I’d use today are the ones I’m building into LensAI, and the Wayfair experience is the raw material for understanding why those patterns matter.

DS analogy — the city that grew faster than its zoning
The Wayfair pricing platform was like a city that grew faster than its zoning laws could adapt. The early districts were planned well for their original purpose. As traffic and population grew, new districts were added adjacent to old ones, inheriting their boundaries rather than rethinking them. By the time the city was large enough to see its own shape, moving a district boundary was a multi-year political project. The platform was technically sound. Ownership: the wrong unit of analysis

The pricing platform was organized around functional teams: Price Output Systems owned Aletheia. Price Scheduler owned Pilgrim. PAS owned the adjustment layer. Price Management owned the run orchestrator. Each team owned their service end-to-end — code, deployment, on-call. That was the explicit design intent and it worked well in isolation.

What it got wrong was the cost of cross-team data contracts. The pricing_refresh Kafka topic had 40 partitions and was consumed by several teams. When Aletheia needed to change the event schema — adding a field for multi-region price differentiation — the change required coordination across three teams, two sprint cycles, and a staged rollout with a schema compatibility window. The schema was not versioned in any formal sense. The coordination cost was invisible in any team’s velocity metric and showed up only as a recurring drag on cross-team feature work.

What I’d do differently: own the schema as a first-class artifact, separate from the service. A schema registry with explicit versioning rules — backwards-compatible additive changes are non-breaking, removal of fields requires a deprecation window — turns cross-team coordination from a meeting to a CI check. The schema becomes the contract, not the service. Teams can evolve their implementations independently as long as the contract holds. The Kafka topic is an infrastructure detail. The schema is the API.

This is the model I’m using for LensAI’s inference event schema. The InferenceEvent fields are documented, versioned, and the ingestion service validates against them at the boundary. Adding resolved_model_id on Day 23 was a backwards-compatible addition: existing consumers saw a new optional field. Removing a field requires a deprecation notice in the schema changelog first. The ownership discipline is designed to prevent the coordination debt I saw at Wayfair.

Observability: cost was invisible

Aletheia’s Bigtable cache served ~20,590 average storefront RPS across 7 production clusters. When a pricing run completed and new prices propagated through Aletheia, the cache hit rate changed. When the cache hit rate changed, the per-request cost changed. Datadog showed the RPS. It did not show the cost per request or the cost per pricing run. That number was known to finance, not to engineering.

The consequence was that engineering optimizations and regressions were invisible from a cost perspective. A new index on a CloudSQL table improved query latency. It also doubled the write amplification during pricing runs, increasing Google Cloud Storage costs for WAL backups. Both effects were real. Only the latency improvement was measured. The cost regression was discovered three months later during a quarterly cloud spend review.

What I’d do differently: cost attribution at the event level, not the billing level. Every pricing run that goes through Delphi should produce a pricing_run_cost_usd estimate at completion time, based on the known cloud resource costs for that compute configuration. Every Aletheia cache miss should tag a counter that approximates the Bigtable read cost in dollars, not just in QPS. Cost becomes an engineering metric, not a finance report.

This is directly the problem LensAI is built to solve for AI inference. The prompt_tokens, completion_tokens, cached_tokens, and the per-token rate card in ClickHouse give you the cost per request. Slice by resolved_model_id and you have cost per model per day. The Wayfair problem taught me that cost visibility must be engineered in, at the data layer, before anyone needs it. Building it after the quarterly review is always too late.

Event sourcing the pricing calculation

Delphi was a stateless pure function: inputs in, retail price out. That design choice was correct and I wouldn’t change it. What I’d change is how the inputs to Delphi were assembled and how the outputs were consumed.

The input assembly happened across several systems: ScheduledReader pulling from multiple Bigtable endpoints, MarginOptimizer providing margin strategy, CompIntel providing competitor target prices, PAS providing adjustments. These were all separate HTTP calls assembled synchronously into a Delphi request. When any upstream service was slow or unavailable, the Delphi call block propagated. The pricing run throughput was bounded by the slowest upstream dependency, not by Delphi’s own compute capacity.

What I’d do differently: materialize the inputs as a joined event stream before the calculation. Each upstream system publishes its latest state to a Kafka topic. A materialization job joins them into a PricingInputEvent keyed by SKU. Delphi consumes the PricingInputEvent stream and is purely compute — no upstream HTTP calls during the calculation. Upstream slowness decouples from calculation throughput. The calculation queue depth becomes the backpressure signal, not a timeout cascade.

The Kafka topic as the join point also gives you something the synchronous assembly never had: a replayable history of pricing inputs. When a price looks wrong, the question “what inputs produced this price?” has an answer stored in the topic, not reconstructed from logs. Debugging a pricing anomaly goes from a multi-hour investigation to a topic offset lookup.

The two-team problem

I led two engineering teams at Wayfair simultaneously during part of my time there. That meant context-switching between two on-call rotations, two sprint cadences, two sets of architectural decisions, and two sets of cross-team dependencies. The practical effect was that neither team got the focused architectural attention they needed for the hardest decisions.

The thing I didn’t understand at the time was that architectural quality is not proportional to the number of engineers on a team — it’s proportional to the number of hours the senior decision-maker spends thinking about the problem before writing code. Splitting those hours across two teams didn’t halve the quality degradation. It squared it, because the cross-cutting concerns between the two teams created dependencies that neither team’s architecture accounted for.

What I’d do differently: one team, one platform. Not because two teams can’t build good systems — they can — but because a single person leading two teams is not leading either team well. The constraints are physical, not personal. Every hour spent in a sprint planning meeting for Team B is an hour not thinking about Team A’s architecture. The ownership model requires sufficient attention from the person responsible for it.

What I owned vs what the broader team owned
The numbers in this post (Aletheia RPS, PAS Core throughput, CloudSQL configurations, GKE cluster topology) describe the broader Wayfair pricing platform, which was built and maintained by multiple teams across multiple years. My contribution was leading two teams within that platform, working on specific subsystems including the pricing calculation pipeline and the adjustment layer. The scale numbers are accurate to the production environment my teams shipped into — they are not a claim that I built those systems from scratch.

The bridge: lensai-dev as an ownership stake

The Wayfair experience taught me that ownership is not a title. It is a specific technical commitment: I will maintain the schema contract, track the cost attribution, and design the system so the next engineer can understand it without archaeology. You can hold that commitment informally, inside a company, as long as you remain there. When you leave, the commitment evaporates unless it was encoded somewhere external.

The lensai-dev GitHub organization is that external encoding. Moving infra-ai-streaming, distributed-flagd, and ebpf-llm-tracer under a shared org creates a public platform identity that outlasts any single employment relationship. The OpenAPI spec I wrote today is a schema contract that is public, versioned, and testable. The integration tests are the contract enforcement that CI runs on every PR. If I leave this project tomorrow, a new contributor can read the spec, run the tests, and understand the ownership boundaries without needing to ask me.

At Wayfair I learned what it costs when platform identity is scattered across team folders in a private monorepo. Building in public, with a real spec and a real test suite, is the ownership model that survives the contributor leaving.
