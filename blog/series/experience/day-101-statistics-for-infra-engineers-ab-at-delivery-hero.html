Day 101 — Statistics for Infra Engineers — A/B at Delivery Hero

We almost shipped a map change on a 200-sample "win" — a lift in on-time delivery rate that looked real until a two-proportion z-test showed it was well inside the range plain noise produces at that sample size.

At Delivery Hero I worked on the Global Logistics Platform, the stack that scaled rider tracking to 1M+ daily orders on AWS EKS. Order Service sits at the center of it: customer and rider ingress, persistence through Order Data Service, and the lifecycle events — PLACED, RIDER ENQUE, RIDER PICKED UP, PICKED UP — that Order SQS carries out to Route Service and Route Consumers. Route Consumers read those events off the queue and hand them to an OSRM cluster, the map-matching engine that turns a rider's raw GPS trail into an actual route, at something like 5,000 route updates a second during peak.

One tuning change on that path was a small adjustment to how often Route Service told OSRM to recompute a rider's route mid-delivery — more frequent recomputation in exchange for more OSRM load. The metric that mattered was on-time delivery: did the order arrive inside its promised window, yes or no. That's a proportion, not a continuous number, which turns out to matter a lot for what happened next.

Scope note — Delivery Hero, Global Logistics Platform
My resume documents the role directly: Senior Software Engineer, Global Logistics Platform, Berlin (Jun 2022–Jul 2023), scaling the platform to 1M+ daily orders on AWS EKS and building end-to-end rider tracking on OSRM processing 5k+ real-time route updates per second. What follows is the general shape of a statistical mistake that kind of small-sample A/B rollout structurally invites — not a specific documented incident, dated experiment, or exact p-value my sources don't itemize.

A Lift That Looked Real at 200 Orders

The rollout ramped the new recomputation setting to a small slice of live traffic first, the way any change touching live routing should. Within a few hours it had covered a couple hundred orders, and the on-time rate in that slice was running a few points above the control group's on-time rate over the same window.

A few points on 200 orders is exactly the kind of number that looks like a launch-worthy result if you only look at the headline percentage. It's also exactly the kind of number a fair coin produces by accident often enough that treating it as proof of anything is a mistake, and nobody on the team had run the actual test that would have told us which one we were looking at.

So what: a percentage-point gap between two groups is not evidence on its own — it's a measurement, and a measurement needs a sample size behind it before "the numbers moved" can honestly become "the numbers moved because of what we shipped."

Why 200 Samples Wasn't Enough

Flip a fair coin 10 times and getting 7 heads doesn't make the coin biased — it's a completely ordinary outcome for a coin that's exactly 50/50, and anyone who has actually flipped a coin ten times in a row knows runs like that happen constantly. The fewer flips you have, the wider the range of outcomes a fair coin can produce just by chance, and 200 orders split across two groups is closer to ten coin flips than it feels like when the number "200" is sitting on a dashboard.

A two-proportion z-test is the tool that turns "does this gap feel real" into an actual answer. It pools both groups' on-time counts into one combined rate, then asks how many standard deviations apart the two groups' individual rates are from that pooled rate — a bigger gap on a bigger sample counts as more surprising than the same gap on a small one, because a small sample has so much more room for pure chance to produce exactly that gap.

The check we should have run before shipping
Pool the two groups' on-time counts into one rate, p_pool. Compute z = (p1 − p2) / sqrt(p_pool · (1 − p_pool) · (1/n1 + 1/n2)). If |z| doesn't clear roughly 1.96, the gap is inside the range noise alone can produce at that sample size — not evidence the change did anything.

So what: the fix isn't a smarter dashboard or a prettier chart — it's running the pooled-proportion math before calling a percentage-point gap a win, because a gap that clears the bar at 200 samples and a gap that's just noise at 200 samples can look identical on a screen.

The Rollout Gate That Should Have Existed

The actual fix isn't a smarter engineer double-checking every dashboard by hand — it's a gate baked into the rollout process itself, one that refuses to call an early read a win until the sample clears a floor and the z-score clears a threshold.

A percentage-point gap and a statistically significant gap are two different claims — and a small sample makes the first one easy to get and the second one easy to fake without ever meaning to.

That gate has to fire before someone writes the launch announcement, not after, because reversing a decision people have already told their manager is a win is a much harder conversation than holding a rollout one more day.

The Same Gate, in Today's Code

Today's llm-judge-eval work (Phase 2A) builds exactly this discipline into DriftWatch's drift check: an is_significant(drop, n) helper implementing the same two-proportion z-test, comparing a current window's judge-score pass rate against a per-segment baseline's pass rate. A judge score is bucketed pass/fail at a threshold, which makes it a proportion in exactly the same sense an on-time delivery flag is a proportion — and a proportion earns the same test regardless of whether the thing being measured is a rider's delivery window or an LLM judge's verdict.

The helper enforces a 30-sample floor on both windows before it will report anything but insufficient_data, and flags real drift only when |z| > 1.96 — the same 200-orders-isn't-enough lesson, encoded so nobody downstream of the drift API has to remember to ask "wait, how many samples was that" before trusting a flagged drop.

So what: a rollout that ramps to live traffic and watches a metric move is doing half the job — the other half is refusing to call that movement a result until a sample-size-aware test says the movement is bigger than noise alone would produce. Whether the proportion in question is on-time deliveries out of a rider's route recompute setting or pass/fail judge scores out of an LLM's response, the same two-proportion z-test and the same 30-sample floor are what stand between "the numbers moved" and "we know why."
