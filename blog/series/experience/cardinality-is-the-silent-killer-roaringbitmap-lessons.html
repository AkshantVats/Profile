Cardinality Is the Silent Killer — RoaringBitmap Lessons

Experience Series · 5 of N

Nobody had shipped a feature. I noticed the Prometheus scrape duration climbing on a Tuesday — checked request rate, flat — and spent an hour convinced the exporter had a memory leak. It didn't. Someone had added pod as a label on a fleet metric the week before, and I didn't find out until I diffed the scrape config.

That one label multiplied every existing tag combination in the index. Scrape cost didn't grow because more users arrived. It grew because the TSDB now tracked thousands of series that hadn't existed seven days earlier — one for every pod value crossed with every existing label value. The index had to store every combination, and nobody had asked whether it could afford to.

Grafana still rendered. The numbers looked fine.

That is the failure mode I keep coming back to: the chart paints green while the truth quietly rots underneath. Same family as the cross-tier percentile bug in When Percentiles Lie — the system stays up, the answer is wrong, and nobody pages because the dashboard looks believable. Cardinality was the upstream cause. We had indexed a cross-product nobody modeled on paper first.

What Cardinality Actually Means

A time series is not "a metric." It is a metric name plus one unique combination of label values. http_requests_total{method="GET", status="200"} and http_requests_total{method="POST", status="500"} are two different series in the index, even though they share a name. This matters because traffic can stay perfectly flat while series count climbs. You didn't add load. You added labels.

Each new label value creates another combination with every value already there. That is the cross-product, and it is multiplicative — not additive. Add model_id with two hundred values to a metric already carrying tenant_id with five hundred values and three statuses, and you are not adding two labels. You are inviting up to 300,000 series for that single metric (200 × 500 × 3).

The cluster does not crash. It gets slower. Then it starts dropping series under memory pressure, so aggregates run on a subset. Your dashboard still loads — the number on screen is computed from whatever series survived, and you have no way to know what's missing.

Cardinality is multiplicative. One label does not add series — it multiplies them.

How WhiteFalcon Indexes Series

Team system

WhiteFalcon at Agoda: Kafka → Rust ingestion → Redis hot tier and Parquet cold tier, with a RoaringBitmap inverted index per tag value. Public write-ups describe indexing hundreds of millions of series at Kafka volumes on the order of 1.8 trillion events per day.

My contribution

I added a moderate-cardinality Kubernetes service tag — modeled the cross-product before merge — and worked on cross-tier quantile alignment (Experience 2). I did not design the RoaringBitmap engine. I learned its constraints by proposing new dimensions and watching the review catch what production would have punished.

The engine works like this: one bitmap per tag value, mapping to the set of series IDs that carry it. Query for model=X AND cluster=Y and the engine intersects two bitmaps. That is fast when each tag has tens or hundreds of values. It becomes catastrophic when a high-cardinality tag lands on a high-traffic metric, because every bitmap grows overnight and every filter now walks a structure nobody budgeted for.

flowchart LR
  K[Kafka events] --> ING[Rust ingestion]
  ING --> HOT[Redis hot tier]
  ING --> IDX[RoaringBitmap per tag value]
  Q[Query: filter model + cluster] --> IDX
  IDX --> SER[Series ID sets]
  SER --> MERGE[Intersect / union]
  MERGE --> HOT
  MERGE --> COLD[Parquet cold tier]
  IDX -.->|high-cardinality tag| BLOW["Bitmap explosion\n→ slow filter, OOM"]

Agoda's public engineering posts explain why inverted bitmap sets beat naive posting lists at hundreds of millions of series — I cite them in the footnotes rather than re-derive the compression math. What mattered in review was simpler: if a tag multiplies series faster than traffic grows, you are not "adding observability." You are changing the cost of every query that touches that metric, whether or not anyone updates the dashboard.

The Spreadsheet Before the Label

When I proposed adding a Kubernetes service-level tag to a WhiteFalcon metric, the review question was not "is this useful?" It was "how many unique values will this tag have in six months, and what metrics already carry it?"

I ran the cross-product on a spreadsheet before the PR was merged. Service names were bounded — a few hundred, stable across deploys. Pod names were not. That single distinction was the entire decision. The service tag landed. The pod tag did not.

The discipline transferred directly to today's inference pipeline. model_id and tenant_id live as ClickHouse columns for analytics — not as Prometheus labels. A columnar store scans dimensions. A label-indexed TSDB indexes every combination. One creates analytical slices you can query on demand. The other creates time series that the index must track forever.

At Agoda we rejected "just add pod" on fleet counters for the same reason. Pod cardinality tracks deployment churn — every deploy adds values that never come back. Multiplying pod against every existing tag is how you turn a one-line Helm change into a quarter of index growth, with no feature launch and no traffic spike to explain it on the incident call.

Where Prometheus Stops

Prometheus is a label-indexed TSDB built for ops: a few hundred series per metric, not hundreds of thousands. It degrades well before the cardinality that AI infrastructure routinely produces. You feel it first as scrape duration creeping. Then memory pressure climbs. Then the TSDB starts evicting series to stay under the limit. Dashboards still load because the remaining series look fine — which is precisely why nobody catches it until the number is already wrong.

What I didn't expect was how invisible the degradation is. There is no error. The scrape succeeds — it just takes longer. Series get dropped — but the dropped ones don't appear in the chart, so the chart looks complete. P99-by-model_id on a Prometheus histogram with unbounded labels is a lie waiting to happen, because you are creating shards the index never meant to track.

Cardinality kills the cluster before traffic does. The dashboard still renders — that is the trap.

The split that transferred from Agoda: Prometheus for bounded ops counters, a columnar store for analytical slices over high-cardinality dimensions. WhiteFalcon did not use ClickHouse. The discipline was the same.

model_id × tenant_id on Today's Pipeline

Each inference event carries model_id, tenant_id, token counts, phased latency, and cost_usd. Those fields belong on the event row, not on a label. The analytical store targets on the order of 10k+ dimension combinations — a design goal, not a production benchmark. A columnar engine can group by model_id without creating one time series per combination. Prometheus cannot.

The P99-by-model panel works because model_id stays a ClickHouse column. If it were a Prometheus label, the histogram would carry every model × tenant × status combination as separate series. The panel would still render — until memory pressure started evicting the long-tail models, and the P99 you trust would be computed from a shrinking subset you never audited.

The producer keys Kafka by tenant_id; the target hash is tenant_id:model_id so one hot model cannot poison a tenant's partition. That is placement, not cardinality — but it comes from the same instinct: bound the blast radius before the noisy neighbor shows up in the dashboard.

How Cardinality Dies Quietly

These failures rarely throw an exception. They change what the index contains while the UI keeps painting green.

  • Scrape lag — series count grows; request rate stays flat; no error spike on the service. On-call has no reason to look.
  • Series limit drops — the TSDB evicts old series to stay under the cap; aggregates run on a subset; P99 can look better and be wrong.
  • Sparse new label — merge paths return nonsense when one shard has data and another does not. Same class as the percentile bug.
  • OOM on compactor — index rebuild crosses a line you modeled in theory but never stress-tested. The compactor restarts; nobody reads the log until the next on-call rotation.
  • "Helpful" pod label — a fleet metric becomes per-pod overnight. The team learns on a Friday when scrape duration spikes and nobody shipped anything.

What Stayed With Me

RoaringBitmap set operations at Agoda scale taught me that observability is an indexing problem before it is a visualization problem. You can ship a beautiful Grafana row on day one and still be lying by day thirty, because nobody multiplied tag cardinalities when the label landed.

The instinct I kept is unglamorous: a spreadsheet before the label. Count the values. Multiply the combinations. Check whether the index can afford the cross-product six months from now. Nobody pages on "scrape duration crept 40 percent with no feature launch." You have to catch it before merge.

Throughput and lag on bounded labels — enough to page when the pipe chokes. Dollars and P99 in the warehouse, where high-cardinality dimensions belong in columns, not labels. Incidents fade. What stays is the discipline of multiplying before you merge.

Traffic growth is visible. Cardinality growth hides inside labels. One shows up on dashboards. The other changes what dashboards mean.
