Building in Public — The Blog Post That Outlives LinkedIn

Architecture posts as durable hiring signals

AS
Akshant Sharma
June 3, 2026
10 min read
Experience Series · 16 of N

A LinkedIn post about shipping a distributed system gets 200 impressions over three days, then falls below the fold. A blog post with the same story — architecture diagram, explicit tradeoffs, honest benchmark numbers — gets referenced in a hiring committee email nine months later.

I noticed this pattern after writing about WhiteFalcon’s storage tiering at Agoda. The LinkedIn version said “shipped cross-tier query optimizations at 1.5T events/day.” The blog version explained how histogram bucket counts enable mathematically correct P99 merges across Redis and Ceph tier boundaries. The LinkedIn post is already gone from feeds. The blog post still surfaces in conversations about distributed storage design.

Platform thinking is reuse, not rewrite. The same applies to writing: a post that documents a real decision becomes a contract you can reference again.

The algorithm versus search

LinkedIn’s algorithm surfaces engagement, not expertise. A post that gets reactions in the first hour stays visible. A post with technical depth but no early engagement disappears regardless of quality. This is the wrong signal for demonstrating engineering judgment at the senior and staff level.

A blog post indexed by search engines has the opposite dynamic. Someone searches “Kafka consumer group rebalancing under high partition counts” six months from now and finds the post where I worked through the rebalance latency problem at Delivery Hero. The algorithm has no part in that discovery. The engineering decision documented in writing is the entire artifact.

The distribution mechanism and the shelf life are the key difference. Social posts optimize for the first 48 hours. Written documentation optimizes for the long tail: people who have the same problem you already solved, arriving months later with the same search intent.

What makes a post referenceable

After 17 posts in this series, the pattern is clear. The posts that get referenced in hiring conversations have three properties that the posts that disappear do not.

First: a real constraint named explicitly. “High cardinality metrics” is a category. “1.5T events per day with 240 million unique series IDs, where standard Prometheus label explosion would exhaust 24GB of heap in under a minute” is a constraint. The specificity is what makes it searchable and verifiable. Anyone who has hit the same constraint recognizes it immediately.

Second: the decision that was rejected. Every real architecture decision involves a road not taken. Documenting why you did not use ClickHouse for hot-tier serving — because HBM latency at P99 is incompatible with the SLO, not because you didn’t know about ClickHouse — tells the reader you actually made the decision under constraint, not under ignorance.

Third: something the reader can reproduce. A command, a benchmark, a schema. Not pseudocode — actual syntax. The reader’s bar for trust rises sharply when they can run the thing and see the output you described.

Pattern from production systems
At Agoda, WhiteFalcon’s internal documentation that survived team turnover always had this structure: constraint + decision + rejected alternative + benchmark. The docs that became folklore were the ones that named the failure modes explicitly, with data.

The documentation contract

Think of a blog post as a contract you sign with a future reader who has the same problem. A bad contract has vague terms: “we improved database performance by redesigning the schema.” A good contract is specific and falsifiable: “migrating cold-tier Parquet compression from Snappy to Zstd reduced storage cost by 15–20% with less than 1% read latency impact, measured on 30-day compacted files at 512MB block size.”

The specificity is both the value for the reader and the proof for the hiring committee. A staff engineer who sees that sentence knows you understand compression tradeoffs, that you measured before and after, and that you know the operating constraint (read latency P99 must not regress). That is three signals in one sentence. A LinkedIn post version of the same work gets none of them across.

The accumulation effect

A single well-written post helps. Seventeen posts in a consistent series does something different. Each post builds the same vocabulary — Kafka, ClickHouse, eBPF, cardinality, latency percentiles — from a different angle. A hiring committee reading three posts in the series sees a coherent technical perspective across multiple problem spaces, not a collection of isolated examples.

This is the platform thinking analogy from the bridge: the Kafka producer I built today for ebpf-llm-tracer reuses the exact InferenceEvent schema I documented in the infra-ai-streaming design posts. The documentation contract I wrote earlier became an integration contract I could implement without rethinking the design. Writing about platform decisions creates reusable artifacts, the same way well-designed interfaces create reusable code.

The accumulation effect works in two directions. Forward: each post creates something the next post can reference. Backward: each new post adds context that makes the older ones more legible. A reader who finds post 17 and reads back through the series gets the full arc: TSDB at Agoda, geospatial tracking at Delivery Hero, pricing engine at Wayfair, and now zero-SDK LLM observability via eBPF. That is a legible engineering biography, not a resume.

The failure modes

The posts that do not survive have one of three failure modes. Vagueness: scale numbers that are obviously rounded or omitted, decisions described without the constraint that drove them. Completeness theater: a post that covers every possible angle but reaches no conclusions — it reads like a survey, not like someone who had to make a hard call. Resume phrasing: “delivered a high-performance system at scale” is a line from a CV, not a sentence from someone explaining what they built.

The test I use: if a senior engineer unfamiliar with the problem could not reproduce the key decision from the post alone, the post is not specific enough. It is not about length — a tight 1,000-word post with real numbers is more referenceable than a sprawling 3,000-word overview without them.

LensAI: writing the contract before the code

For LensAI, the documentation pattern has a practical consequence. The InferenceEvent schema — model_id, tenant_id, latency_ms, prompt_tokens, source — was defined in writing before the Kafka producer existed. Today’s Day 17 code implemented exactly that schema without design discussion because the documentation contract was already signed.

This is how production infrastructure works at Agoda, Wayfair, Delivery Hero: the schema and the integration contract are decided in writing first. The code is the implementation of a decision that was already made. Writing first is not documentation overhead — it is the mechanism by which distributed teams can build independently against the same contract.

Platform thinking is reuse, not rewrite. Blog posts, like good API contracts, are the artifact that enables others — and your future self — to build without re-litigating every decision from scratch.
