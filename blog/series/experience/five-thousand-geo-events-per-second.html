The support ticket said the rider was on the wrong street. The map showed him three blocks away. Both came from the same pipeline.

Nothing was on fire. No 500s. Just geometry that did not match what support expected during dinner rush — the same kind of quiet wrong I wrote about at Walmart and Agoda: dashboards green, decision wrong.

From 2022 through 2023 at Delivery Hero in Berlin, I led the end-to-end rider tracking system — the geospatial pipeline that turns GPS noise into a map customers and support can trust. I built it and rolled it out on the Global Logistics Platform across international markets: separate brands and regions under one platform, each with its own traffic shape but shared services. That is multi-company, multi-tenant in practice — not one monolith order book.

What follows is my scope on that stack. I am not claiming I ran every order in the Delivery Hero Group. Public numbers below are company context; the 5k/s and 1M+ figures are what I operated and what my resume states.

The System I Was Debugging at 8pm on a Friday

Friday pages rarely start in OSRM. They start between order state and map work.

Customers and riders hit Order Service, backed by Order Data Service. When an order moves — placed, picked up, rider assigned, rider en route — the service publishes to Order SQS: PLACED, PICKED UP, RIDER ENQUE, RIDER PICKED UP. Riders publish here too. That queue separates "what happened to the order" from "what we compute on the map."

Route Consumers read those messages and call Route Service, which talks to an OSRM cluster on top of a road-graph store. OSRM does the CPU-heavy matching; trip state lives elsewhere. The handoff to apps and support is Route JSON — polyline, distance, metadata. When someone asks "where was the rider?", the Revisit Order System replays immutable snapshots. Order Service also feeds GBQ for analytics. I owned the hot path: stream shape, map-matching, and keeping Route JSON honest under load.

  RID[Rider] --> OS
  RID --> SQS[Order SQS\nPLACED · PICKED UP\nRIDER ENQUE · RIDER PICKED UP]

Routing work stays async and tied to lifecycle events, not every GPS tick. Geometry still runs hot at peak — but the queue tells you why we asked for a match.

Five Thousand Per Second — What Counts as an Event

On the pipeline I led: 5,000+ geo-events per second at peak — GPS fixes, map-match calls, route updates, and notifications, often more than one event per fix when raw and matched coordinates both flow. That is not 5,000 orders per second.

The logistics platform in my scope handled 1M+ daily orders across those markets. Delivery Hero Group globally is a different, larger number — about 10 million orders per day in published figures,1 with a 10.4 million single-day record in December 2024.2 Both belong in the story; they measure different things.

Partition Keys and Dinner Rush

We keyed the geo stream by rider_id and market so one courier's fixes stayed ordered. Map-matching needs that order. It also creates hot shards: one rider circling a block in the rain, or a staging lot full of couriers, and one partition falls behind while the fleet average still looks fine.

Dinner rush does not move the mean much. It moves the tail. When support is staring at one late order, a global P99 is the wrong chart.

Key for correctness first. Then watch lag per shard — that is where refunds come from.

Order SQS — When We Recompute a Route

Not every GPS point should trigger map work. The queue carries order life:

  • PLACED — baseline expectations.
  • PICKED UP — merchant handoff; geometry changes.
  • RIDER ENQUE — courier assigned; matching picks up.
  • RIDER PICKED UP — en route; customer ETAs tighten.

Route Consumers turn those into Route Service calls. Miss RIDER ENQUE and you match the wrong trip. Process the same message twice and support may see two Route JSON distances — they believe whichever rendered last.

SQS is at-least-once. That is why we needed idempotent writes and immutable snapshots in Revisit — same reason duplicate GPS is dangerous without dedupe.

OSRM Under Load

OSRM /match snaps GPS to the road graph. In production it is a cluster on a shared graph build, not a dev container. Under dinner rush the scary failure is not always a timeout. Sometimes you get a confident wrong match — a clean polyline on the wrong parallel road.

When OSRM p95 drifted, Route Service shed non-critical recomputes, coalesced fixes per rider, and refused to let queues grow without showing lag. Public single-node benchmarks are tens of matches per second; they explain why we queue — they are not the capacity plan.3

Labels Before You Trust a Percentile

A fleet P99 without region, map_dataset_rev, and client_version describes someone else's night. Map releases change matches. App versions change GPS sampling. We put those fields on the span every time — not as optional Grafana filters.

When Nothing Pages

  • Duplicate GPS — replay inflates distance; ETAs lie, no 500.
  • Hot shard — one partition lags; the mean still looks fine.
  • Missing map_dataset_rev — you cannot tell graph drift from a code change.
  • Clock skew — fixes arrive out of order; support sees time travel.

Same Question on Today's Kafka Topic

On AI Learning Day 5 I key inference events by tenant_id and model_id so one customer's stream stays ordered. At Delivery Hero I keyed geo by rider_id and market for the same reason. Different payload — same design question: who shares fate when load spikes?

Get the key wrong and one hot city or one hot model looks healthy on a cluster chart while a single partition falls behind. I still watch per-shard lag before I trust a global percentile — whether the buffer is Kinesis, SQS, or Kafka.

What Stayed With Me

Incidents fade. What stays is the contract between order event, matched geometry, and what support is allowed to show. Leading rider tracking across markets taught me that five thousand events per second are not solved by bigger boxes. They are solved by honest scope, the right partition key, and Route JSON you can replay when dinner is late.
