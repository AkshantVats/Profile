Day 65 — Token Budgets — Finance Meets Gateway

A supplier's cost change and a tenant's token spend are the same shape of problem: a number that can run away, guarded by a rule that has to choose between blocking outright and doing nothing at all — unless someone designs a third option.

Today's ticket was a design doc, not a code change: cost-budget-enforcer, a per-tenant sliding-window cap on LLM token spend. No incident forced this one — it's a guardrail being designed ahead of the traffic that would need it, the same way a finance control gets written before the first supplier tries to game it. That's worth saying plainly, because the two systems I'm about to compare are separated by eighteen months and an entire domain, and the honest link between them is a shared shape of problem, not a shared outage.

The shape is this: a number that a third party controls and that can grow past what the business can absorb, guarded by a rule that has exactly two easy answers — block everything past the line, or don't enforce a line at all — and both easy answers are wrong for symmetrical reasons.

The Two Easy Answers, and Why Both Are Wrong

A hard reject-only guardrail is the simplest thing to build: define a ceiling, block anything past it, done. It also fails in public the first time it fires, because "done" means the caller gets nothing — no partial service, no degraded mode, just a wall. For a pricing platform where a supplier's cost submission feeds live storefront prices, or an LLM tenant whose product depends on an answer coming back, a hard wall at the ceiling turns a spend problem into an availability problem.

A silent no-op — log the overage, enforce nothing — is the opposite failure. It protects availability perfectly and the budget not at all. Whoever asked for the cap in the first place, finance or a platform owner, gets a number that was never actually a limit, just a suggestion nobody could see was being ignored until the bill or the incident review showed up.

So what: a guardrail that only knows "allow" and "block" is choosing between an availability failure and a budget failure, and the honest fix is a design that doesn't force that choice at all.

What a Real Cost Guardrail Looked Like at Wayfair

I led the Pricing Adjustment (PAS) and Pricing, Promotions & Discounts teams at Wayfair, and the systems those teams owned sat downstream of one specific kind of third-party-controlled number: a supplier's submitted base cost. Suppliers update their own cost figures through the Unified Cost Management System (UCMS), and those figures propagate into the pricing engine's total-cost calculation — which means a submitted cost that's wrong, or a jump that's implausible, is exactly the "third party controls a number that feeds your system" problem, just denominated in dollars-per-SKU instead of tokens-per-request.

Team scope — Wayfair, Sr. SWE III, PAS & Pricing Promotion teams, Nov 2024 – Mar 2026
UCMS tracks supplier base-cost changes with a dedicated violations table and change log — a cost submission that crosses a configured threshold is flagged rather than either silently accepted or hard-blocked at the API layer. The bulk cost-change pipeline processing those submissions was team-owned infrastructure serving 20,000+ suppliers.
My contribution
I architected the GCP event-driven Global Pricing & Promotion Engine that reduced price propagation from hours to sub-seconds across those 20k+ suppliers, and built the high-throughput bulk processing framework — with distributed rate limiting and circuit breakers — that kept that pipeline at 99.99% availability under peak load. The guardrail-vs-availability tradeoff in today's design doc is the same one that framework had to make on every cost change flowing through it.

The violation flow doesn't reject a flagged cost change outright — a legitimate cost increase (a real supply-chain shock, say) has to be able to land, just with more scrutiny than a routine update. And it doesn't silently accept it either, because an unflagged, unbounded cost jump feeding straight into the pricing engine is exactly the failure mode a finance control exists to prevent. The answer UCMS lands on is a third state between the two: flagged-but-not-blocked, visible to whoever reviews it, distinct from both the routine path and an outright rejection.

So what: the same organization that needed sub-second price propagation also needed a guardrail on the input feeding that propagation — speed and a cost cap were never in tension, because the cap was designed to degrade the review path, not the propagation path.

Three Thresholds, Not One

Today's cost-budget-enforcer design doc makes the same three-way split explicit, denominated in tokens instead of dollars-per-SKU:

ThresholdWayfair analogcost-budget-enforcer action
Early warningCost change flagged in violations tableWebhook fires at 80% of budget; traffic unaffected
Soft limitFlagged change routed to manual/extra review, not blockedNew requests route to a cheaper fallback model
Hard limit(No direct analog — UCMS never fully blocks a submission)Requests rejected with HTTP 429 (Too Many Requests) + Retry-After header

The one place the analogy breaks is instructive rather than incidental: UCMS never has a true hard-block state, because a supplier's cost figure, however implausible, is still information the business needs to see and adjudicate — discarding it outright isn't an option the domain allows. An LLM token budget has no equivalent obligation to "see" the request that would have gone over budget; rejecting it costs nothing the domain values. So the hard limit exists here specifically because the two domains diverge on what a rejection actually loses, not despite the parallel — a design borrowed from one context still has to be checked against what's different in the new one, not copied wholesale.

So what: the shared shape is "warn, then degrade, then only block as a last resort" — where the two systems diverge is exactly where the cost of a rejection diverges, and that divergence is a design decision, not an oversight.

What Transferred, and What Had to Be Rebuilt

What transferred cleanly: the instinct that a guardrail's job is to protect the budget without making the system unavailable, and that doing both requires more than one threshold. What didn't transfer at all: the mechanism. UCMS's violation flow is a human-review queue — a flagged cost change waits for a person.

cost-budget-enforcer's soft limit has to resolve in the same request's latency budget, with no human in the loop, which is why it routes to a cheaper model instead of a review queue. Same philosophy, completely different implementation, because a synchronous inference gateway and an asynchronous supplier-cost pipeline have different latency contracts on the thing that has to happen once a threshold is crossed.

A finance control and a rate limiter solve the same problem — the mechanism that resolves it has to fit the latency the domain actually allows.

So what: porting a design pattern across domains means keeping the part that's actually general (three thresholds, not two) and rebuilding the part that isn't (what "degrade" means when there's no human in the loop to ask).
