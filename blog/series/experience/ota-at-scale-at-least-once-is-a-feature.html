OTA at Scale — At-Least-Once Is a Feature, Not a Bug

Walmart · firmware · Kafka offsets in disguise

AS

It is rollout day for a refrigerated fleet. Millions of devices live behind flaky Wi‑Fi and intermittent cellular, and the firmware image has to land somewhere eventually — even when the network lies about “success.” Exactly-once OTA to edge hardware is a fiction; the engineering question is whether duplicate delivery is safe (idempotent apply) or poisonous (the wrong version applied twice with side effects).

In OTA terms, the ordering invariant is simple: the control-plane “ack” must be coupled to the data-plane becoming true. If the device acks before a verified flash (and before idempotent apply), every downstream story—rollback, SLOs, and anomaly attribution—becomes guesswork.

The problem textbooks mislabel

Most textbooks treat OTA like reliable delivery: if the cloud sees HTTP 200 (or a “delivered” event), the story ends. In reality, intermittent networks split the world into retries, partial receptions, and out-of-order arrivals. Transport success is not application success; it is just “something might have happened.”

That’s why I keep the same mental model I use for Kafka consumer offsets today: commit after side effects are safe, not after the packet arrived. OTA is similar—your “commit point” is the moment the device has verified the image and can safely ignore replays.

Before and after: push OTA vs staged, acked rollout

Before (push-and-pray): the cloud uploads an image, the device reboots on the first successful fetch, and there’s no durable ack. When connectivity drops during the critical window, you get silent partial rollout: the fleet state is unknowable until users complain or telemetry finally catches up.

After (staged, acked rollout): we treat the rollout like a state machine. A staged manifest gates the target version, the device downloads and verifies, and only then do we record a durable “device is on version X” ack. Retries are capped; devices that fail N times move into a quarantine lane so they stop poisoning aggregates and restart storms.

In our WeIoT SmartBuildings work (Aug 2018 – May 2021), this staged approach mattered because we managed 7M+ sensors and tens of millions of telemetry points per minute on Azure IoT Hub + Stream Analytics. The rollout tooling didn’t just push firmware; it owned the state transitions that kept analytics honest.

At-least-once is a feature when apply is idempotent

At-least-once is not an apology. With edge hardware, duplicates are cheaper than lost firmware—a missed update is a functional failure, while a replay can be detected and safely ignored.

Idempotency keys in disguise are the real mechanism: device_id + target_version + image_hash. The device applies the update once per unique triple, verifies the hash, and treats replays as a no-op. The ack store records that durable apply decision; duplicate acks are allowed because they confirm the same state.

Think of OTA as a safe-commit system: the ack happens after verified flash, not after the first HTTP fetch. That ordering is the difference between retrying delivery and accidentally re-running a side effect.

Intermittent networks change the SLO

Once you accept intermittent delivery, your SLO has to describe the rollout as an eventual state transition, not a single “all devices in one hour” promise.

SLO shift: from “all devices updated in 1 hour” to “99.9% fleet on target version within 72h; remainder quarantined.” The rest of the time, the system is doing controlled retries, not silently stalling.

Proof metrics are about where devices get stuck: ack rate by region, stuck-on-version counts, quarantine depth, and median time-to-ack under flaky RSSI. If quarantine grows, you stop pretending the rollout is healthy.

Rollback is explicit: manifest pointer revert plus blocking the bad version at the edge gateway, so the next retry wave is directed away from poison state.

Finally, retries need a cap. Intermittent networks don’t justify retry storms—cap retries, back off, and alert on the slope of quarantine growth.

Edge filtering upstream (bridge to today)

The earlier IoT work taught the hard lesson: when you ingest poison telemetry, you don’t just add noise—you make aggregates wrong. The safest fix is to quarantine bad identity and bad state at the edge, before it turns into rollups.

Today’s inference telemetry needs the same shape. The z-score anomaly detector on latency_ms per model_id (sliding window 100, flag when >3σ, warm-up skip when σ=0) emits structured anomaly events to the ai_anomalies topic and increments anomalies_detected_total. The operational point is upstream: catch the bad behavior before it contaminates “normal” time-series.

The anomalies topic is where exact rules end — semantic cache false positives will land in the same operational queue.

What I'd ship differently now

Azure IoT Hub era tooling was the right shape for that job; Kafka-native inference observability is a different plumbing layer. I wouldn’t pretend the components are interchangeable. The lesson that transfers is the design constraint: treat retries as a state machine with explicit “safe commit” points and a poison-message lane.

So if I were shipping OTA today, I’d frame at-least-once like a consumer group: acknowledge only after idempotent apply, and isolate poison via quarantine so it can’t contaminate aggregates. Like Kafka consumer offsets, “success” is the point where the side effects are safe to replay.

One operational punchline

At-least-once is only a bug when apply isn’t idempotent. The fix isn’t fewer retries; it’s explicit verification, durable acks, and quarantine lanes wired to the same operator queue.
