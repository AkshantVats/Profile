Day 48 — Inject Timeout — Chaos for Tool RPCs

At Wayfair, a circuit breaker protecting bulk price updates from 20k+ suppliers only earns its keep if it trips correctly before a slow dependency turns into a cascading outage — and nobody gets to wait for a real SPCS failure to find out. traceforge replay --inject-timeout forces an agent's tool call to fail the same way, on command.

The failure mode I actually worried about at Wayfair was never "SPCS is down." It was "SPCS is down, and the system protecting us from it has never once been tested against a real outage, because real outages don't schedule themselves for a convenient afternoon."

A circuit breaker you've never watched trip is a circuit breaker you're hoping works. The only way to know before it matters is to trip it yourself, on a system that isn't actually broken, and watch what happens downstream.

20k+ Suppliers, One Circuit Breaker Away From a Bad Day

At Wayfair, I led the PAS and Pricing, Promotions & Discounts teams and architected a GCP event-driven Global Pricing & Promotion Engine that cut price propagation from hours to sub-seconds across 20k+ suppliers. Suppliers submit bulk price and promotion updates — up to 250k+ SKU updates per supplier, in near real-time — and every one of those submissions eventually calls out to SPCS — Supplier Cost Service, the downstream dependency that computes and validates net cost for each part of the batch.

My role — Wayfair, Bengaluru, Nov 2024–Mar 2026, Sr. Software Engineer III, Led PAS & Pricing Promotion Teams
I owned the high-throughput bulk processing framework behind this pipeline — distributed rate limiting and circuit breakers that kept it at 99.99% availability by eliminating degradation and cascading failures under peak global load. The chaos-testing pattern below is how that circuit breaker's trip condition got verified, not a tool I shipped as a product feature.

A supplier submission that hits a slow or failing SPCS doesn't get to hang forever. The pipeline retries the call a fixed number of times with a fixed delay between attempts and, if SPCS is still failing after that, marks the task ERROR and the project FAILED — an internal alert email (Graymatter) fires and an audit event (Scribe) is logged, and the supplier or an admin has to resubmit. That retry-then-fail boundary is the circuit breaker's whole job: stop hammering a struggling dependency and fail fast instead of letting one slow service back up 20k+ suppliers' worth of traffic behind it.

So what: the retry count and the fixed delay between attempts aren't just tuning knobs — they're the exact line between "SPCS is having a bad minute, and we absorbed it" and "SPCS is having a bad minute, and now so is everyone submitting a price update."

The Bridge: You Can't Wait for a Real Outage to Verify the Breaker

Verifying that boundary against a live SPCS outage means waiting for SPCS to actually go down, on a day nobody picked, and hoping the monitoring catches everything that happens next. That's not a test plan, it's a bet that the next real outage happens to be instructive instead of just damaging.

An agent's tool calls have the identical problem, at a smaller and more debuggable scale. A recorded trace only ever shows what a tool call actually did — almost always succeed — so the path an agent takes when a tool times out or comes back with a 500 is invisible to replay unless you force it to happen.

So what: "wait for the real failure to test the failure path" isn't a strategy at either scale — it's the same gap, whether the thing behind the breaker is a supplier price submission or a single tool call inside an agent run.

What Today's Code Enforces: Fail the Step, on Purpose

The new pkg/fault package in agent-replay-engine defines three synthetic failures — timeout, HTTP 500, stale cache — and ToolMocker.Inject(atStep, kind) forces a chosen tool-call step to fail with one of them during replay, regardless of what actually happened when that call was originally recorded. traceforge replay --log run.jsonl --trace-id abc --inject-timeout 6 forces the 6th tool call in the trace to fail with a synthetic timeout, and the replay's error-handling path runs exactly as if a live timeout had hit.

That is a chaos test in miniature: cause the failure deliberately, on your own schedule, against a system that would otherwise never show you what its unhappy path actually does.

20k+suppliers behind the pipeline
250k+SKU updates per supplier
99.99%availability, rate limits + breakers
1step forced to fail, on command

So what: a breaker that trips correctly against a synthetic failure is the same breaker that trips correctly against a real one — the synthetic version is just the one you get to run on a Tuesday afternoon instead of during an incident.

Testing a Breaker Without Cutting the Power

An electrician verifying a circuit breaker doesn't wait for a genuine short circuit somewhere in the building. They isolate the circuit and trip the breaker deliberately, watching whether it interrupts the current the way it's supposed to and whether everything downstream fails safe instead of catching fire. The building's power stays on the whole time — only the one circuit under test ever sees the fault.

ToolMocker.Inject does the same thing to one tool call inside a recorded trace. Every other step in the replay runs against its normal, successful recorded response — only the chosen step sees the synthetic failure, isolated the same way the electrician isolates a single circuit before testing it.

You don't learn whether a breaker works by waiting for a fire. You learn it by tripping the breaker yourself, on a circuit you chose, before the fire has a chance to ask.

The Ticket That Stopped Being a Guess

At Wayfair, confidence in the circuit breaker came from deliberately forcing SPCS-down conditions in a controlled environment and watching the retry-then-fail path actually execute — not from reading the retry configuration and assuming it would behave as documented under real load. The difference between those two is the difference between a system you've verified and a system you've merely described.

--inject-timeout exists for LensAI and TraceForge's version of that same discipline: an agent's error-handling code is only real once someone has forced it to run, not just read it and trusted the happy path was the only path that mattered.
