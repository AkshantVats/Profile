Day 24 — BigQuery Streaming vs Batch — Burst Traffic Truth

Wayfair · PAS analytics · pas-analytics Pub/Sub — what slot exhaustion taught me about trusting “real-time” analytics

AS
Akshant Sharma
June 10, 2026
13 min read
Experience · Day 24 of 150

I first noticed the gap in a weekly business review. The pricing indices dashboard — built on BigQuery over the pas-analytics Pub/Sub pipeline — was showing numbers that lagged reality by thirty minutes. Not missing data, just old data. Everything downstream of PAS Core was technically fine. The events had arrived in Pub/Sub. BigQuery could see them. The dashboard just could not see them yet.

The cause was a full-catalog pricing run. Pilgrim had kicked off a sweep, Delphi had processed millions of price updates, and PAS Core had published a corresponding burst of analytics events to pas-analytics. The streaming insert path into BigQuery had hit its slot quota. Under normal load the streaming path was invisible — events appeared in BigQuery seconds after being published. Under the burst of a full-catalog run, the streaming path silently degraded to batch behavior while the dashboard continued showing a “real-time” data freshness indicator.

This post is about the difference between streaming and batch in BigQuery when your traffic profile is not flat — and why treating “real-time analytics” as a given is a mistake that only reveals itself during burst.

DS analogy — the restaurant ticket window
A restaurant kitchen handles steady dinner service comfortably: each ticket arrives, the line handles it, plates go out in five minutes. Then a tour bus pulls in and drops off sixty tickets at once. The kitchen can’t cook sixty meals in parallel. Plates queue up. The “estimated wait: 5 minutes” sign on the door is still technically true — it just no longer applies. BigQuery streaming inserts under burst are the kitchen. The slot quota is the number of stoves.

The streaming insert assumption

BigQuery streaming inserts are exactly what they sound like: you write a row, it appears in query results within seconds. The Pub/Sub to BigQuery pipeline using streaming inserts feels like a distributed log with a SQL query layer on top. For moderate, predictable traffic, it works like that.

The assumption buried inside streaming inserts is that your slot allocation can absorb the insert rate. BigQuery slots are the unit of compute capacity for processing data — for inserts, for queries, for the internal machinery that makes data queryable after it arrives. Slots are pooled across all operations in a project. A pricing run that triggers millions of Dataflow batch jobs, hundreds of ad-hoc analyst queries, and a burst of streaming inserts simultaneously is not the use case the dashboard team was modeling when they built the freshness indicator.

The PAS analytics pipeline published events to pas-analytics Pub/Sub in Avro format after every price adjustment operation. During normal operation — ~3,414 RPS on PAS Core — the streaming insert rate was well within quota. During a full-catalog Pilgrim run, PAS Core hit ~15,263 RPS and the analytics event rate followed. The streaming insert path was not designed for 4.5x burst; the slot allocation was sized for normal operation.

What happens when slots exhaust

BigQuery does not fail gracefully when streaming inserts hit slot pressure. The insert operation returns success — the row is accepted into an unprocessed buffer — but the row does not appear in query results until the buffer flushes. The buffer flushes when slots become available. During a pricing run with simultaneous Dataflow pipelines consuming most of the project’s slots, that flush could be delayed by twenty to forty minutes.

The dashboard saw a valid streaming insert response and showed the data freshness indicator as green. The data was not stale from the pipeline’s perspective — it was sitting in BigQuery’s internal buffer, successfully accepted, waiting for compute. From the business stakeholder’s perspective, the dashboard was lying about how current the pricing indices were.

What the Wayfair PAS team built
The pas-analytics Pub/Sub pipeline, the Dataflow pipelines running GBQ-to-CloudSQL and GBQ-to-Bigtable, and the BigQuery dataset schema were built and owned by the PAS engineering team. I worked on monitoring the pipeline’s health during pricing runs, identifying the slot exhaustion pattern through Datadog traces and BigQuery INFORMATION_SCHEMA queries, and the recommendation to classify analytics writes by urgency. The slot allocation and quota decisions were infrastructure team scope, not PAS team scope.

The slot budget problem

The underlying issue was not BigQuery’s design — it was that the pricing platform had never explicitly modeled analytics write traffic as a resource class with its own slot budget. Dataflow batch pipelines were provisioned against estimated job sizes. Analyst queries had their own reservation. Streaming analytics inserts were assumed to be “cheap” relative to the batch jobs and were not allocated a dedicated reservation.

That assumption held during normal operation. It did not hold during a Pilgrim full-catalog run when Dataflow pipelines processing millions of rows, analyst teams running ad-hoc pricing queries, and the pas-analytics streaming inserts were all competing for the same shared slot pool simultaneously.

The fix was not to switch entirely to batch. It was to separate the analytics write traffic into two urgency classes. Events that fed the WBR dashboard — the weekly business review numbers executives looked at — were written via a dedicated streaming reservation that was not shared with Dataflow batch jobs. Events that fed longer-running analysis had a 10-minute acceptable delay and used the Datastream path, which had an explicit data_freshness of 600s by design. The routing decision was made at publish time based on the event’s destination topic tag.

Batch is not slow, it is honest

What I took from this is a preference for explicit latency contracts over implicit freshness claims. The Datastream path — PostgreSQL via Datastream into BigQuery with a 600-second data freshness — was designed around that contract. The schema for the source CloudSQL databases had explicit change-data-capture configuration. The Datastream connector documented the lag. Everyone who built dashboards on top of that path knew the latency.

The streaming insert path was implicit about its latency under burst. The API returned 200 and callers assumed queryability. The mismatch between what the API promised and what the system delivered under slot pressure was invisible until someone noticed a number in a WBR that was thirty minutes stale.

DS analogy — the post office vs the courier
A courier promises delivery in two hours. A courier under a city-wide mail surge promises delivery in two hours but actually delivers in six. The post office says “up to three days” and delivers in two — because its contract is honest about the worst case. Batch analytics is the post office. Streaming analytics under burst is the overpromising courier. The business stakeholders watching the WBR dashboard are the recipient waiting by the door.

The batch path for the less time-sensitive analytics — the Dataflow pipelines processing millions of rows per pricing run — was not a limitation. It was an honest acknowledgment of what the system could sustain. The pipelines ran, they completed, the data appeared in BigQuery with a lag that was known and accounted for. Nothing broke. Nothing silently delayed. The latency was a parameter, not a surprise.

The bridge: what this means for flag audit events

The pas-analytics Pub/Sub topic is what I think of when I look at the audit log in distributed-flagd. The flagd audit log is an append-only topic of flag change events — when a flag was created, updated, enabled, disabled, and by whom. Every flag evaluation that changes behavior produces a record in the audit log that is independent of etcd’s current state.

The lesson from pas-analytics is that the audit topic should be owned by the service, not by the consumer. PAS analytics worked reliably when the pipeline’s slot allocation was controlled at the publisher level — when PAS Core decided which events went to which reservation. The flag audit log in distributed-flagd follows the same principle: the flagd service writes audit events unconditionally, and consumers (dashboards, compliance reports, replay tools) read from the log at their own pace.

Append-only, replayable, owned by the publisher. The architecture that made pas-analytics reliable under burst is the same architecture that makes a feature flag audit log trustworthy. So that the compliance team can ask “what was the model rollout percentage for tenant X at 14:32 UTC” and get an answer, even if etcd has been compacted since.

What I took from this system

The most durable lesson is that “real-time” is a latency class, not a binary. A system that delivers data within ten seconds under normal load and within forty minutes under burst is not a real-time system — it is a variable-latency system with a misleading label. Designing for the burst case means naming the worst-case latency in the SLA, allocating resources against that worst case, and making sure dashboards reflect actual freshness rather than last-write time.

The second lesson is about resource reservation. Shared slot pools are a reasonable default for a single team’s workload. They become a footgun when multiple teams — Dataflow batch, analyst queries, streaming inserts — share the same pool without coordination. The pricing platform eventually moved to reservation-based slot allocation for the most latency-sensitive analytics writes. The move was not dramatic, but it made the WBR dashboard reliable by construction rather than by luck.

Building distributed-flagd, I keep coming back to the same question: what is the worst-case latency for this operation, and have I made it explicit? The CRD reconciler syncs to etcd — that sync has a latency. The audit log has a flush interval. The gRPC watch stream has reconnect latency. None of these are streaming-insert style hidden surprises, because each one is documented as a parameter with a known worst case. The PAS analytics incident is the reason I write that documentation first.
