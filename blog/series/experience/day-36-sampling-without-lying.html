Day 36 — Sampling Without Lying

Head-only sampling makes your SLO dashboard lie. At Agoda’s 1.5T-events-per-day scale, I learned why you must tail-sample every error — the alternative is a P99 that hides the failures your users feel.

When Agoda’s WhiteFalcon team first explained the ingest pipeline to me, the number that kept coming up was 1.5 trillion events per day. That is the volume the Rust consumers read from Kafka and write into Redis hot tier. Not requests. Not API calls. Events — one per metric sample, across every service, every host, every Grafana panel refresh. Storing all of them at full resolution forever is not a choice anyone makes.

Sampling is the decision you make about which events to keep. Get it wrong, and the events you discard are exactly the ones your SLOs are trying to measure.

What head sampling actually does to your data

My scope at Agoda — WhiteFalcon
Contributing engineer for ~5 months on the WhiteFalcon time-series database. The Agoda team designed the core sampling and storage architecture. My contributions were in the cross-tier query engine and a new cardinality dimension for Kubernetes infrastructure tags. I did not design the Kafka pipeline or the Rust consumer layer.

Head sampling picks a random fraction of incoming events and discards the rest. At 10% rate, you keep roughly 150 billion events per day from WhiteFalcon’s ingest volume. Your storage bill drops by 90%. Your dashboards still show trends. Your P50 and P95 still look right.

Then someone asks why their hotel search returned a timeout. You look at the error trace. It isn’t there. It was in the 90% you discarded.

Here is the math that makes this dangerous: if your error rate is 0.1% and you head-sample at 10%, your stored error rate drops to 0.01%. That is not a smaller version of the real error rate. That is a number that triggers no alert, feeds no SLO burn calculation, and appears in no oncall dashboard. Your P99 latency looks clean because the slow outliers — which are usually errors — never made it into the sample.

The SLO integrity problem

Non-software analogy — the quality inspector
Imagine a factory quality inspector who checks one in every ten products. That is head sampling. Now imagine that defective products are slightly smaller and fall through a gap in the conveyor before the inspector arrives. The inspector never sees them. The defect rate in the inspector’s report is zero — not because defects aren’t happening, but because the sampling mechanism systematically excluded them. Your SLO percentage is the inspector’s report.

SLOs measure the fraction of requests that succeed within a latency budget. An SLO of 99.9% availability means at most one in a thousand requests can fail in a given window. If you head-sample that window at 10%, you see one in ten thousand failures in your data. Your burn rate calculation is off by a factor of ten. You think you have 100 error budget minutes remaining. You actually have 10.

The SLO integrity problem is not a logging problem or a metrics problem. It is a sampling strategy problem. The fix is not to keep more events — you cannot afford that at 1.5T per day. The fix is to change which events you keep.

What tail sampling corrects

Tail sampling inverts the head sampling decision for a specific subset of events: instead of sampling randomly, you look at the outcome first and keep all events that signal a failure. Every error, every timeout, every retry. You still head-sample the happy path — the 99.9% of requests that complete normally — and keep just 10% of those. But the 0.1% that fail? You keep all of them.

The storage savings are almost identical to pure head sampling in practice, because errors are rare. At 0.1% error rate, tail sampling forces you to keep 0.1% of all events unconditionally. That is 1.5 billion events per day — a rounding error on the 150 billion you’d keep anyway at 10% head rate. Your total storage is essentially unchanged. Your error data is now 100% complete.

Why errors concentrate in the tail

There is a second reason to tail-sample errors that is less obvious than SLO integrity. Errors are not uniformly distributed across request latency. They cluster in the tail of the latency distribution. A request that times out takes longer than a request that succeeds. A request that triggers a retry is at least twice as long as a request that does not. The events you would discard under pure head sampling are disproportionately the events that inflate your P99.

This is why P99 looked too good at Agoda when I first read the dashboards. The compaction jobs ran on hourly windows. Within a window, if 10% of events were sampled randomly and errors clustered in the slow requests, the P99 of the stored sample excluded most of the slow tail. Not all of it — some slow requests succeeded — but enough that the P99 from the stored data was 15–20% lower than what the alert threshold was calibrated to.

From lesson to code

Today’s traceforge/pkg/sampling package implements this strategy in two types. HeadSampler takes a rate between 0 and 1 and uses FNV hashing on the trace ID to make the decision deterministic per trace — all spans in a trace share the same fate, so you never get a partial trace in storage. ErrorTailSampler keeps any span with status error, retry, or timeout unconditionally.

CombinedSampler wires them together with an OR decision: if either the head or tail sampler says keep, the span is kept. At 10% head rate, a trace where all spans are ok has roughly a 10% chance of being stored. A trace with even one error span is stored in full, because the tail sampler fires on that span and keeps all spans sharing its trace ID.

// DefaultCombined returns the production strategy:
// 10% head + 100% error/retry/timeout tail.
func DefaultCombined() *CombinedSampler {
    return &CombinedSampler{
        Head: &HeadSampler{Rate: 0.10},
        Tail: &ErrorTailSampler{},
    }
}

The collector now exposes /metrics/sampling in Prometheus exposition format: traceforge_spans_kept_total, traceforge_spans_dropped_total, and traceforge_sampling_effective_rate. At 0.1% error rate, the effective rate on a production workload should read approximately 10.09% — 10% from head sampling plus the 0.09% tail contribution from errors. If the effective rate ever reads significantly above that, your error rate is higher than your SLO budget assumed.

PII scrubbing must run before sampling

There is one sequencing constraint that is easy to get wrong. PII scrubbing must run before the sampling decision, not after. If you sample first and scrub only the kept spans, you have created a path where PII in a dropped span never touched your scrubber — but the span was still decoded and processed in memory by your collector before being dropped. Whether that constitutes a compliance boundary violation depends on your data classification policy. The safer design is to scrub every span before any sampling decision, so PII never survives past the ingest boundary regardless of sampling outcome.

Tail sampling costs almost nothing in storage. The events you must keep unconditionally are the rare ones — errors, retries, timeouts. The savings come from the happy path, which is exactly where you can afford to sample.

The lesson from WhiteFalcon is not specific to time-series databases. Any sampling layer that discards errors is building a dashboard that actively hides the failures your users feel. Head sample the happy path. Tail sample the expensive path. The two strategies cost the same in storage and produce completely different answers about system health.
