Day 60 — Semantic Caching Economics

Threshold vs savings curve. Sweep 0.88–0.96 on real prompts and plot dollars, not vibes — a false-positive cache hit and a false-negative cache miss don't cost the same amount, so the threshold that maximizes hit rate is rarely the one that maximizes savings.

Sweep 0.88 to 0.96 on real prompts. Plot dollars, not vibes.

A semantic cache doesn't decide whether two prompts are "similar enough" in the abstract — it decides that at a specific cosine-similarity threshold on their embeddings, a vector representation of a prompt's meaning that lets you compare two different sentences by how close their vectors sit in space. Pick the threshold by eyeballing a hit-rate chart and you've picked a number that feels right. Pick it by pricing out what a wrong cache hit actually costs, and the number moves — usually higher than intuition suggests.

The Scanner Dial, Not a Slider

DS analogy — an airport scanner's sensitivity dial, not a volume slider
Turn a baggage scanner's sensitivity up and it catches more real threats, but flags more innocent bags for a manual search — a cost paid in agent time. Turn it down and the line moves faster, but a genuine threat has a better chance of passing through unflagged — a cost paid in an entirely different currency, one that's not measured in minutes. A similarity threshold is the same dial: it doesn't trade speed for speed, it trades one kind of cost for a structurally different kind.

A semantic cache's threshold is exactly that dial. Set it low (loose matching) and more prompts count as "close enough" to a cached entry, so more requests get served instantly instead of hitting the LLM — more savings, but also more chances that "close enough" was actually "different enough to matter." Set it high (strict matching) and you only reuse an answer when you're nearly certain the two prompts meant the same thing — safer, but you pay full LLM price far more often.

So what: a similarity threshold isn't a single knob you tune for "accuracy" — it's a dial that trades a cost you can graph (LLM spend) against a cost you usually can't, until you deliberately go measure it.

Why the Two Costs Aren't Equal

A false negative — a cache miss on a prompt that actually had a good cached answer waiting — costs you exactly one wasted LLM call. That's a bounded, known number: the price of one generation. A false positive — a cache hit that serves a cached answer to a prompt that meant something meaningfully different — costs you a wrong answer delivered with the same confidence as a right one, and a wrong answer's downstream cost is whatever it takes to notice, explain, and undo the damage it causes.

That asymmetry is the entire reason the threshold sweep needs two curves instead of one. A hit-rate chart alone treats every additional cache hit as a pure win, because it can't see the difference between a hit that saved money and a hit that quietly served the wrong answer to someone who trusted it.

OutcomeWhat it costsIs the cost bounded?
True positive (correct hit)Zero — the LLM call was avoidedYes, known in advance
False negative (missed hit)One LLM call, paid instead of avoidedYes, known in advance
False positive (wrong hit)A wrong answer served with full confidence, plus whatever it takes to catch and fix thatNo — depends entirely on what the prompt was for

So what: when one branch of a decision has an unbounded downside and the other two don't, the threshold that "looks optimal" on a symmetric hit-rate curve is biased toward the wrong side by construction — you have to price the asymmetry in on purpose, or the chart will hide it.

Sweeping the Threshold — Illustratively, Not Yet Measured

Here's the shape of that sweep, worked through with illustrative round numbers rather than measured production traffic — there's no live embedding model wired into this design yet. Say an LLM call costs $0.002 and a caught false positive costs 50x that in support and regeneration overhead, roughly $0.10.

ThresholdIllustrative hit rateIllustrative false-positive rateNet $ per 1,000 prompts
0.88 (loose)~55%~2.0%Savings mostly erased by false-positive cost
0.92~40%~0.4%Clear net savings
0.94 (design default)~30%~0.08%Best net savings in this illustration
0.96 (strict)~15%~0.01%Safer, but savings shrink faster than risk does

semantic-cache-engine's DESIGN.md sets a default of 0.94 with a false-positive budget of 0.1%, which sits close to where this illustration's net-dollar curve peaks — not because 55% hit rate wasn't tempting, but because the false-positive cost at 0.88 eats most of what the higher hit rate appears to save.

So what: the threshold that maximizes hit rate (0.88) and the threshold that maximizes net savings (near 0.94) are different numbers in this illustration, and only one of them is the number worth shipping — the sweep exists precisely to find out which.

Decay Is the Same Curve, Moving Over Time

A cached entry's safe threshold isn't constant over its own lifetime. A response cached an hour ago is answering a question about the world as it was an hour ago; a response cached 29 days into a 30-day TTL is answering a much older question, and the odds that "close enough" has quietly become "no longer true" go up the longer that entry sits. Tightening the threshold as an entry ages is the same dial from the airport-scanner analogy, turned up gradually as the bag has been sitting on the belt longer.

So what: a single fixed threshold is really a snapshot of the correct threshold at entry age zero — treating it as correct at age 29 days is the same mistake as treating a hit-rate chart as the whole cost picture, just moved from one axis to the other.

What This Post Doesn't Prove

The honest gap: every number in the sweep table above is illustrative arithmetic, not a measurement. There's no embedding model or pgvector index running against real prompt traffic in this sandbox yet, so the actual hit-rate and false-positive curves for semantic-cache-engine are still unmeasured — the 0.94 default is a design target taken from the DESIGN.md, not a number this post's sweep produced.

So what: "the shape of the tradeoff is asymmetric" is a claim this post earns with arithmetic; "0.94 is the right threshold for this system" is a claim only a real sweep against real traffic can earn, and conflating the two is exactly the mistake the false-positive column above is warning against.
