Day 112 — Alert Deduplication — State Machines

State keyed by rule_id+tenant. Yesterday's escalation list said what should happen once a rule fires; today's evaluator is the state machine that decides whether "firing" is even the right word for this tick, and whether anyone still needs to hear about it.

State keyed by rule_id+tenant. Not global, and not per-rule either — a noisy tenant flapping a shared rule has to be silenceable without silencing a different tenant's genuine first alarm on that exact same rule, which means the debounce clock has to live one level more specific than the rule itself.

Two Questions a Threshold Can't Tell Apart

Yesterday's CompiledRule gave a rule an ordered escalation list — step 0 at zero delay, step 1 at thirty minutes, and so on. A naive evaluator would walk that list by asking one question every tick: is the condition true right now? If yes, notify.

That's not enough, and the gap isn't obvious until a metric flaps. A condition that goes true, false, true, false dozens of times a minute re-enters "true" every single time it flips back — and a one-question evaluator re-notifies on every re-entry, because "is it true" and "have I already told someone about this" are different questions with different answers.

So what: an evaluator that only tracks the condition's current value has no way to distinguish a brand-new alarm from the fortieth re-appearance of the same one in the last two minutes — it needs a second axis, time since last notification, that the condition's boolean value alone can never supply.

The Mechanism, Two Stages

Stage one is the familiar half: a condition has to hold continuously for a configured duration — the rule's for field — before it's trusted enough to fire at all. This is pending-before-firing, the same sustained-true idea a Prometheus for field already captures, and it throws out a single noisy sample on its own.

Stage two is the one yesterday's design didn't build: once firing, each escalation step is only allowed to notify if the segment hasn't notified more recently than a configured debounce window — thirty minutes, per today's ticket. And critically, when the condition clears, the pending/firing progress resets, but the debounce clock does not — it has to survive exactly the resolve-then-refire cycle a flapping signal creates, or the clock is worthless the moment traffic gets noisy.

DS analogy — a crosswalk push-button
Pressing a crosswalk button fifty times doesn't summon fifty walk signals — the first press arms the request, and every press after that until the signal actually cycles is a no-op, because the intersection already knows a pedestrian is waiting. Each intersection has its own independent button state; mashing the button at Main & 1st does nothing to whether Main & 2nd's signal is armed. A segment's debounce clock is that button: one press (notification) arms it, and further presses (re-triggers) do nothing until the cooldown actually elapses.

So what: debounce isn't "ignore the condition for a while" — it's a clock that tracks when a segment last spoke, kept deliberately separate from how many times the underlying condition flickers past its threshold in the meantime.

The Concrete Result

Modeled as a test: a metric flapping true/false across a threshold roughly every 1.5 seconds for ten minutes — 400 potential re-triggers, the exact shape today's Experience post reports from a real Kafka consumer-lag alert. Run through the evaluator with the thirty-minute debounce window in place, and the segment notifies exactly once.

200+Notifications, dedupe window = 0

Disable the debounce window entirely (a test written specifically to prove the first result isn't passing by accident) and the same 400-tick sequence produces well over a hundred notifications — every true-tick that follows a false-tick re-enters "firing" and re-sends. The difference between those two numbers is the entire value of stage two.

So what: this isn't an abstract claim about a debounce window being "a good idea" — it's a specific, re-runnable before/after count, and the fact that the without-dedupe test exists at all is what makes the with-dedupe result trustworthy rather than assumed.

The Honest Limitation

A thirty-minute window is a chosen tradeoff, not a provably optimal one. Set it too wide and a second, genuinely distinct alarm that happens to land inside that window gets silently absorbed along with the duplicate noise it exists to suppress — from outside the system, a correctly-debounced storm and a wrongly-swallowed second incident look identical: quiet.

Nothing about this state machine tunes that number for a given rule automatically. Thirty minutes came from the ticket, not from a measured distribution of how long a real rebalance-shaped flap actually lasts, and a rule with a much noisier or much quieter failure mode than this one would reasonably want a different window.

So what: dedupe trades a small, real risk of swallowing an early second alarm for a much larger reduction in duplicate noise, and that tradeoff has a dial, not a single correct setting.

The Experience Bridge

Today's Experience post reaches this from the operations side: a real consumer-lag alert on Agoda's Kafka pipeline fired 400 times in about ten minutes during a routine rebalance, fixed by adding exactly this shape of debounce to the alert's config. Same underlying gap, two different vocabularies for closing it.

An alert that can tell "still true" from "still the same episode" is the difference between a routine, self-healing operational event and a paging incident of its own making.

So what: the fix in both systems wasn't a bigger alert budget or a quieter Slack channel — it was giving the alerting layer a notion of identity and time it didn't have before, and that notion is exactly what a small per-segment state machine is for.
