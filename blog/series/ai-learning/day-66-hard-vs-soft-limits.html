Day 66 — Hard vs Soft Limits

Block vs downgrade. Soft limit routes to a cheaper model — the same peak-demand instinct behind a delivery platform charging more instead of simply refusing new orders when demand outstrips capacity.

AS
Akshant Sharma

7 min read

Soft limit routes to a cheaper model — the same instinct behind a delivery platform raising the price on a ride or an order instead of just turning riders away when every driver in a zone is already busy. Neither system treats "demand exceeds capacity" as a single yes/no gate. Both treat it as a range, with more than one thing that can happen depending on how far past capacity the moment actually is.

Why One Threshold Can't Do a Rate Limiter's Whole Job

A limiter with exactly one cutoff has to pick a single number and live with both of that number's failure modes at once. Set the cutoff conservatively and you warn (or block) long before there's any real problem, wasting headroom the system never needed to give up. Set it aggressively and by the time the limiter fires, the thing it was supposed to prevent has mostly already happened.

There's no single value that's simultaneously "early enough to matter" and "late enough to not be noise," because those are two different jobs, and a single number can only optimize for one.

Today's cost-budget-enforcer implementation makes that explicit by refusing to collapse the job into one number. Three thresholds, each doing exactly one job: 80% of budget fires an early warning with zero traffic impact; 100% routes new requests to a cheaper fallback model instead of the tenant's configured one; 120% rejects outright with a 429 and a Retry-After. Three separate jobs, three separate triggers — nothing is asked to do double duty.

DS analogy — a restaurant kitchen at capacity, not a doorman with a headcount
A doorman with a single headcount limit has one lever: let the next party in, or don't. A kitchen running past its comfortable ticket volume has a middle option a doorman doesn't — it can keep seating tables and switch to a shorter, faster express menu, serving everyone something instead of turning the next party away outright. Only when the kitchen is genuinely out of capacity, not just busy, does it actually stop taking new tickets. cost-budget-enforcer's three thresholds are the same three stages: comfortable, degraded-but-served, and actually full.

So what: a system that can only say "allow" or "deny" is modeling capacity as binary when it usually isn't — there's almost always a middle state where degraded service is strictly better than either full service or none, and a single threshold has no way to express that middle state at all.

Why the Hard Limit Isn't Just the Soft Limit Plus a Margin

The gap between the soft limit (100% of budget) and the hard limit (120%) isn't padding — it's runway, sized on purpose. If the hard limit sat at exactly 100%, the fallback-routed traffic the soft limit is supposed to enable would have nowhere to run: the instant a tenant crosses into "route to the cheaper model," they'd also already be at the hard block, and the softer failure mode would never actually get exercised in practice. Twenty points of headroom is what lets the cheaper-model path absorb a burst's tail before the hard stop actually engages.

This is the same reasoning a delivery platform's demand-based pricing runs on, one level removed from the exact mechanism: when demand approaches a zone's capacity, the response isn't an immediate hard cutoff of new orders — it's a degraded-but-served state (a higher price, a longer estimate) that keeps the system serving requests while demand is elevated, with an actual capacity ceiling still sitting further out as the real backstop. The specific lever differs — price versus model quality — but the shape of the decision is the same three-state machine: comfortable, degraded, full.

So what: sizing the gap between "start degrading" and "actually stop" is itself a design decision with a concrete purpose — too small and the degraded state never gets used; too large and the hard limit stops meaning anything close to the configured budget.

The Threshold Comparison Is the Easy Part

Once a weighted token count exists (yesterday's post covered how that count itself is computed), mapping it to one of four actions is a single switch statement — the hard part was never the decision logic, it's making sure the count feeding that decision is correct under concurrent access. pkg/enforcer.Check in today's implementation is almost entirely branching on a percentage; every line of actual complexity lives one layer down, in the atomic Redis script that produces the percentage in the first place.

That split — cheap decision logic on top of an atomicity-hardened count — is a pattern worth naming on its own, because it's easy to over-engineer the wrong half. A four-way threshold switch doesn't need to be fast or clever; it needs to be obviously correct, which a plain switch over ordered comparisons is. The place that actually earns careful engineering — a Lua script, tested against a real interpreter, exercised with twenty concurrent goroutines — is the one place a race would silently produce a wrong number.

So what: not every part of a guardrail deserves equal engineering effort — the decision that consumes a number can stay simple precisely because the number itself was made trustworthy somewhere else, and conflating the two is how a codebase ends up over-testing the easy part and under-testing the hard one.

What Today's Tests Prove, and What They Don't

Unlike yesterday's design-only day, today's implementation is actually tested — 19 tests, all passing, run against miniredis's real Lua interpreter rather than a mock, plus a twenty-goroutine concurrency test confirming the atomic script closes the race a naive read-then-write would leave open. That's a meaningfully stronger claim than "the design should work" — the Lua script itself executed, the UTC-midnight rollover happened on a real (if embedded) Redis-compatible engine, and the threshold math produced the exact actions the design specifies at each boundary.

What it doesn't prove: how the weighted-count approximation behaves under a real, skewed production traffic pattern against a live Redis instance under network latency, or whether 20 points of soft-to-hard runway is the right number for actual tenant burst shapes rather than a reasonable-sounding default. miniredis is a faithful single-process Lua interpreter, not a network hop, not a Redis cluster under load, and not real traffic.

So what: "the algorithm and the atomicity are correct" is a claim today's test suite actually earns; "the specific thresholds are right for real traffic" is a claim only production data can make, and that gap is exactly why the alert threshold exists at 80% instead of assuming the design got the numbers right on the first guess.
