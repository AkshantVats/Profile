Day 96 — Shadow Traffic — Ethics and Sampling Bias

A non-representative 1% can lie with a straight face — no errors, no crashes, just a quietly wrong answer to the one question the whole pipeline exists to ask. Document the bias, or the sample decides what "good" means without telling anyone.

Project blog concept: when shadow misleads. Day 94's measurement answered a narrower question than that hook implies — it proved mirroring costs nothing (a 0.018ms p99 delta against a 5ms budget), not that what gets mirrored is a fair sample of production traffic. Those are two separate claims, and only the first one got tested with a number attached.

Zero Cost Isn't Zero Bias

DriftWatch exists to answer one question: is the model RouteIQ is currently using still the right one, compared against a shadow model running the same traffic. That question only gets answered honestly if shadow-eval's sample looks like production traffic. Proving Capture is cheap says nothing about whether the sample it produces is representative — those are orthogonal properties of the same pipeline, and DESIGN.md's own §5.2 addendum names four specific reasons the second one can fail while the first holds perfectly.

DS analogy — the daytime landline survey
A phone survey that only calls landlines during business hours gets a response rate that looks completely healthy — people pick up, nothing errors out, the data pipeline is clean end to end. What it silently excludes is everyone who works a daytime job, everyone under thirty who never had a landline, everyone whose answers might have changed the conclusion. Nothing about the survey's own metrics would ever tell you that.

So what: a sample that looks clean by every metric the collection pipeline reports can still be systematically blind to exactly the cases that matter most — and unlike a crash or an error rate, nothing in the pipeline signals that anything is wrong.

Four Gates Between Production Traffic and shadow-eval

Day 91-93's implementation has four specific mechanisms, each traceable to a real line of code, that quietly narrow who gets sampled before the configured shadow_percent ever gets applied. None of them throw an error. Each one just makes the sample a little less like production.

  REQ["Production traffic"] --> G1["Gate: chain-exhausted"]
  G1 --> G2["Gate: buffer backpressure"]
  G2 --> G3["Gate: cold-cache sampling"]
  G3 --> G4["Gate: size truncation"]
  G4 --> EVAL["shadow-eval topic"]

Gate 1 — chain-exhausted requests never enter. mirrorShadowTraffic returns early when X-Model-Id is empty — no production response, nothing to compare against. That means every request where the fallback chain ran out of hops is structurally invisible to shadow-eval, and those are precisely the requests DriftWatch's whole premise cares most about catching.

Gate 2 — backpressure drops correlate with the worst moments. Mirror's bounded channel drops and counts under a slow or dead Kafka broker, exactly as DESIGN.md §2 requires. The effective sampled fraction dips below the configured rate precisely during broker degradation or traffic spikes — the same windows where comparing a routing decision under stress against a shadow model matters most.

Gate 3 — a new tenant samples at the wrong rate. PercentSampler.resolve returns a 1% default for any tenant not yet in its cache, and warm-up is asynchronous. A tenant configured at 50% is sampled at 1% until warm-up completes, and drifts again for up to 30 seconds after any config change — so a canary rollout under-samples exactly the window it's trying to observe.

Gate 4 — large responses get compared truncated. A 1MiB cap keeps mirroring's memory bounded, the same "bounded, not unbounded" rule the channel itself follows. Any completion over that cap reaches the evaluator truncated, biasing disagreement rate upward specifically for the longest completions — the same tail most likely to contain the drift DriftWatch was built to catch.

Every one of these four gates is DESIGN.md's own bounded-drop, bounded-memory rule working exactly as specified. The rule that makes the system safe to run is the same rule that makes its sample non-random — safety and representativeness were never the same property, even though both came from the same design decision.

Today's Demo Caught Gate 2 Live, By Accident

Phase 2A's cmd/demo wasn't written to illustrate sampling bias — it was written to prove Capture never blocks. It ended up demonstrating Gate 2 anyway, with real numbers: 2000 concurrent Capture calls against a 256-slot buffer and a 1ms simulated publish delay dropped 87.0% of events; against a 32-slot buffer and 5ms delay, 98.3%.

Those drop rates are artificially high on purpose — the demo manufactures backpressure that a live Kafka broker at normal load wouldn't produce — but the mechanism is exactly Gate 2, and the demo is proof it fires under real concurrent load, not just in a unit test's controlled handshake. A production incident that spikes both request volume and Kafka latency at the same time would trigger the identical gate, at whatever rate the buffer and the broker's actual slowness produce.

Why "Nothing Errored" Is the Dangerous Case

An outage is easy to notice — dashboards go red, someone gets paged. A silently biased sample produces the opposite experience: captured_total climbs steadily, publish_errors_total stays near zero, every health check is green. The evaluator that eventually reads shadow-eval has no signal, on its own, that four specific classes of request are underrepresented in what it's scoring.

So what: the fix isn't eliminating these four gates — DESIGN.md §2's bounded-drop rule is correct and non-negotiable for production safety. The fix is treating DroppedTotal, cache-warm-up state, and truncation counts as first-class inputs to how confidently the evaluator's eventual verdict should be trusted, not as internal plumbing metrics nobody outside this package looks at.
