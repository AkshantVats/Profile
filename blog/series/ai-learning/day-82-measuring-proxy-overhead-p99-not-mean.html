Day 82 — Measuring Proxy Overhead — p99, Not Mean

Histogram buckets for added milliseconds in a streaming proxy, not a running average — because a mean of 2ms and a p99 of 40ms describe two completely different proxies, and only one of them is the one your slowest 1% of users actually experiences.

Histogram buckets for added milliseconds in a streaming proxy. That's the entire measurement decision this post argues for — and the reason it needs an argument at all is that the obvious alternative, a running average of added latency, is the wrong tool for the exact claim fallback-chain's DESIGN.md §4 makes: under 10ms of added proxy latency.

Yesterday's design committed to that number. Day 82's executor doesn't have a proxy to measure yet — that's Day 83's work — but the measurement discipline has to be decided now, before there's a real number to misreport with the wrong statistic.

Why a Single Average Hides the Requests That Actually Fail

A mean is one number computed by summing every request's added latency and dividing by the count. That arithmetic is exactly why it's a bad fit for a latency claim: a handful of very slow requests and a large number of fast ones can average out to a number that describes almost none of the actual traffic.

Picture 1,000 requests where 990 add 1ms of proxy overhead and 10 add 4,000ms — a garbage-collection pause, a slow DNS lookup, whatever the cause. The mean comes out to roughly 40ms. That number is a fair description of zero real requests: it's higher than what 99% of users experienced and far lower than what the unlucky 1% actually waited through.

So what: a mean answers "what did the arithmetic produce," not "what did a user experience" — and a proxy overhead claim exists specifically to describe user experience, which is exactly the question a mean is built to obscure.

From One Number to a Distribution: p50, p95, p99

A percentile answers a sharper question: sort every request's added latency from fastest to slowest, and ask what value sits at a given position in that sorted list. The 50th percentile (p50, the median) is the point where half of requests were faster and half were slower — a much more honest "typical" than a mean, because it isn't dragged around by a handful of extreme outliers.

p95 and p99 push further into the tail: p99 is the value below which 99% of requests fall, meaning exactly 1% of requests were slower than it. That's the number an SLO (service-level objective, a target a system commits to meeting) usually gets written against, because an SLO's whole job is to bound the worst case a real user can hit, not to describe the typical case.

1msp50, healthy proxy
4msp95, healthy proxy
<10msp99 target (DESIGN.md §4)
40msMean, same synthetic traffic

Those four numbers can all describe the same traffic sample, and only one of them is the number DESIGN.md §4 actually committed to. Reporting the mean instead of the p99 wouldn't just be a rounding difference — a mean of a few milliseconds could hide a p99 that blows straight through the <10ms budget, and every downstream reader would think the target was met.

So what: "under 10ms" is a claim about a specific point in a distribution, and reporting anything else — even an honest, correctly-computed mean — answers a different question than the one the target asks.

The DS Analogy: A Restaurant's Average Wait Doesn't Help the Table That Waited an Hour

DS analogy — the restaurant's posted wait time
A restaurant that tells you "average wait: 12 minutes" is telling you almost nothing about whether tonight is the night you wait an hour. If nine tables out of ten get seated in 5 minutes and one waits 75, the average still comes out around 12 — a number no actual party experienced. A restaurant that instead tracks "95% of parties seated within 20 minutes" is making a promise you can actually hold it to, because it's a statement about the worst case most people will hit, not an arithmetic artifact of blending the lucky and the unlucky together.

So what: the restaurant's average wait and a proxy's mean added latency fail for the identical reason — both blend a large number of fine outcomes with a small number of bad ones into a figure that represents neither.

Computing an exact percentile would mean keeping every single latency value in memory and sorting the whole set on demand — workable at low volume, expensive at RouteIQ's traffic. A histogram sidesteps that: latency values are sorted into fixed buckets (0-1ms, 1-2ms, 2-4ms, and so on, widening at the tail where precision matters less) and only a per-bucket counter needs to be kept.

A percentile then becomes a bucket lookup instead of a sort: walk the buckets from the bottom until the running count crosses 99% of all requests, and that bucket's range is the p99. The buckets also merge cleanly across proxy replicas — summing two histograms' matching buckets produces the correct combined histogram, the same property a Kafka partition count needs when RouteIQ aggregates across replicas elsewhere in the arc.

So what: a histogram is what makes "compute a percentile across millions of requests, cheaply, and mergeable across replicas" possible at all — a running sum literally cannot answer a percentile question, because a mean has already thrown away the distribution's shape by the time it's computed.

Today's Experience Post, Same Shape

Today's companion Experience post covers Delivery Hero's OSRM pool saturating during lunch rush — an 8% error burn that a system-wide average success rate would have partly hidden. A 99.x% overall success rate, averaged across the full day, still describes a real hour where 8% of requests failed outright.

The lesson is the same one this post argues for a proxy's latency: a metric aggregated too broadly, whether across time-of-day or across a distribution's whole shape, can be true and still fail to describe the one interval or the one percentile where things actually broke.

So what: "measure the tail, not just the summary" turned out to be the same lesson at two different layers of today's work — a lunch-rush hour hidden inside a daily average at Delivery Hero, and a p99 hidden inside a mean at RouteIQ.

What Comes Next

Day 83's streaming proxy is what finally gives this histogram discipline a real number to report: time-to-first-byte through the proxy, bucketed and compared against a direct call to the same endpoint, with p50/p95/p99 all reported side by side rather than collapsed into one mean. Only then does DESIGN.md §4's <10ms target move from a committed claim to a measured one.

So what: today's decision is the part worth trusting first, because the actual p99 number is still unmeasured — what's real today is the discipline that keeps tomorrow's number honest once it exists.
