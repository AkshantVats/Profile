Day 18 — Quantization and Model Optimization

GPTQ vs AWQ vs bitsandbytes — pick variants like a codec picks compression

AS
Akshant Sharma

14 min read
AI Learning · Day 18 of 150

I spent the first week of this series treating quantization as a model researcher’s problem. Then I realized it shows up in Kubernetes manifests as GPU memory limits.

The YAML diff between a 7B model deployment and a quantized 7B deployment is one line: the instance class. A 7B model in FP16 needs 16 GB of VRAM. In GPTQ INT4 it needs roughly 5 GB. That gap is an A100 vs. an A10G — a 60% reduction in GPU cost — and the decision that enables it is made in a deployment config, not in a research notebook.

Quantization is a deployment flag, not a training concern. The infra team owns the cost-latency-quality matrix. The choice between GPTQ, AWQ, and bitsandbytes is less like choosing a research technique and more like choosing a compression codec for a message queue: you set the parameters at deploy time, you benchmark the tradeoff, and you ship the config.

What quantization actually is

A neural network’s weights are floating-point numbers. A typical 7B-parameter model in FP32 takes roughly 28 GB of memory. Most GPU instances cap at 24 GB. That gap — between the model’s ideal precision and the GPU’s physical capacity — is the practical problem quantization solves.

Quantization maps high-precision numbers (FP32, BF16) to lower-precision formats (INT8, INT4). FP32 to INT8 is a 4x memory reduction. FP32 to INT4 is an 8x reduction. The mapping introduces approximation error — how much error, and where it concentrates, is the entire science of the field.

Think of a high-resolution photograph. FP32 is the RAW file: every pixel’s value stored exactly. INT8 is JPEG at quality 80 — perceptually identical to most viewers, 10x smaller on disk. INT4 is JPEG at quality 30 — noticeably blocky if you look closely, but usable for a thumbnail. Which level you pick depends on whether you’re printing a billboard or sending a thumbnail — and which compression algorithm you use determines how bad the artifacts are at the same file size.

What I didn’t expect
The accuracy drop is not linear with precision reduction. Going from FP32 to INT8 often costs less than 1% on benchmark tasks. Going from INT8 to INT4 naively can cost 5–15%, but smart quantization algorithms (GPTQ, AWQ) bring that INT4 drop to under 2%. The algorithm matters more than the bit width.

The three approaches — overview

GPTQ (Frantar et al. 2022) is post-training quantization (PTQ). You quantize after the model is fully trained using a small calibration dataset. Compute-intensive to quantize — typically 20 minutes per 7B parameter model on a single A100 — but the quantized model loads and serves fast. It’s a one-time expensive compression step.

AWQ (Lin et al. 2023) is also PTQ, but uses activation statistics to prioritize which weights matter most. A small fraction of weights — roughly 1% — are “salient”: they correspond to large activation values. AWQ preserves precision on salient weights and aggressively quantizes the rest. It’s typically faster to quantize than GPTQ and often produces better quality on reasoning tasks like code generation and mathematics.

bitsandbytes quantizes dynamically during the forward pass. Pass load_in_8bit=True or load_in_4bit=True to from_pretrained() and the model loads quantized with zero offline cost. The tradeoff is inference speed: dequantization happens on the critical path, making it 20–40% slower than GPTQ or AWQ for the same model. Its primary production use case is fine-tuning with QLoRA, not batch inference throughput.

Think of three video compression strategies. GPTQ is pre-encoding your library in H.265: expensive upfront, fastest playback. AWQ is adaptive H.265 encoding that preserves detail in the scenes viewers spend most time on. bitsandbytes is real-time transcoding: flexible, zero setup, but burns more compute per stream.

GPTQ: post-training quantization via OBQ

GPTQ is based on Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ). When you quantize one weight, you introduce error. You can compensate by adjusting remaining unquantized weights using second-order information — specifically the Hessian of the loss. GPTQ approximates this efficiently for transformer layers by processing column blocks sequentially.

In practice: run the model forward on a small calibration dataset (128–512 samples), collect activation statistics, then quantize layer by layer. The output is INT4 weight matrices plus per-group scaling factors in FP16 that add roughly 3% overhead. Those scaling factors are the dequantization constants — the map from INT4 back to the weight’s original range.

Think of how a studio engineer sets EQ for a room. They play a reference track that represents the room’s acoustic characteristics, then tune the EQ settings to the room. GPTQ’s calibration dataset is the reference track. The model is the room. You’re tuning the compression to the signal you actually serve.

Infra implications
GPTQ quantization is a one-time offline job. A 7B model takes roughly 20 minutes on a single A100 at INT4; a 70B model takes about 3 hours. The quantized model is a separate artifact — version it separately from the FP16 checkpoint. Inference requires a CUDA kernel for INT4 weight storage with FP16 accumulation: auto-gptq and exllamav2 provide these.

GPTQ is the right choice when you need maximum inference throughput and have time for an offline quantization job. The inference overhead of GPTQ INT4 vs FP16 is near zero on memory-bandwidth-constrained GPUs like the A100 and H100 because you’re spending less time moving data off the GPU than you save in VRAM reduction.

AWQ: activation-aware weight quantization

AWQ starts from the same insight as GPTQ — not all weights are equally important — but arrives at a different conclusion. Instead of using second-order Hessian information, AWQ uses activation statistics directly. The weights that correspond to large activation values in the forward pass are the ones where quantization error costs the most quality. Scale those salient weights up before quantization (reducing their relative error), then scale them back down at inference. The scaling factors are absorbed into quantization constants.

AWQ typically produces better perplexity than GPTQ at the same bit width on reasoning tasks. The gap is most visible at INT4 and in model families with heavy activation spikes like Llama-2 style architectures. AWQ quantization is also 2–4x faster than GPTQ for the same model size because it skips the layer-by-layer OBQ optimization step.

Think of a piano tuner who prioritizes the middle octaves because that’s where most sheet music lives. GPTQ tunes all strings equally. AWQ listens to the repertoire first, then tunes the strings that get played most. Both produce a tuned piano; AWQ’s piano sounds better on the music you actually play.

When AWQ beats GPTQ
Reasoning tasks: HumanEval, MATH benchmarks — AWQ wins by 1–3 perplexity points at INT4. Quantization time is 2–4x faster. AWQ-quantized models are slightly larger than GPTQ equivalents due to scaling factor storage, but the quality-per-bit is better for workloads that require logical reasoning.

bitsandbytes: runtime quantization

bitsandbytes quantizes weights dynamically during the forward pass. No offline calibration job, no separate artifact, no special toolchain — pass load_in_4bit=True to from_pretrained() and the model loads in NF4 (Normal Float 4-bit) format. NF4 is a non-uniform quantization grid designed specifically for the normally distributed weight values that transformers have. It packs more information into 4 bits than a standard INT4 grid by allocating more precision to the center of the weight distribution.

The primary production use case for bitsandbytes is fine-tuning via QLoRA. You load the base model quantized via bitsandbytes 4-bit, keeping the base weights frozen, and add trainable LoRA adapters in full precision on top. The base model’s VRAM cost drops 8x; only the adapter parameters need full precision. This turns a 70B fine-tuning job from a 4×A100 job into a 1×A100 job.

A portable Bluetooth speaker vs. a home theater system. bitsandbytes is the Bluetooth speaker: works anywhere, zero setup, not the right answer for audiophile quality. GPTQ and AWQ are the home theater: requires installation, optimized for the space it lives in. Neither is wrong; they serve different use cases.

The decision matrix

Three variables determine the right choice: time-to-deploy, inference throughput, and accuracy retention. Each method optimizes for a different combination of them.

If you’re iterating, fine-tuning, or haven’t committed to a model: bitsandbytes. Zero offline cost, any model in one line of config. Accept the throughput hit in exchange for iteration speed.

If you’re deploying to production and throughput matters most: GPTQ. Best tokens per second at INT4, dedicated inference kernels, good quality with calibration. Pay the 20-minute offline job once, amortize it over months of serving.

If you’re deploying to production and reasoning accuracy matters more than peak throughput: AWQ. Same memory footprint as GPTQ at INT4, better benchmark quality on code and math. Quantization job is faster too.

What I didn’t expect
The right choice also depends on the GPU. INT4 with bitsandbytes on an A10G is slower than FP16 — dequantization overhead outweighs memory savings on that GPU generation. GPTQ’s INT4 kernels are optimized for RTX 4090, A100, and H100, where memory bandwidth is the bottleneck, not compute. Benchmark on your target GPU before committing to a method.

Infra implications: what this looks like in a manifest

A quantized model needs less GPU memory. Llama-3 8B in FP16 uses roughly 16 GB. In GPTQ INT4 it uses about 5 GB. That creates two choices: fit the model on a cheaper GPU class (A10G 24 GB instead of A100 40 GB, roughly 60% cost reduction), or fit three replicas on the same A100 for higher throughput per dollar.

The deployment manifest changes only at the instance class level. resources.limits.nvidia.com/gpu: 1 stays unchanged. The finance impact is enabled by an infra decision made in the model serving config — not in the training pipeline.

Model serving frameworks like vLLM and TGI accept a --quantization flag: --quantization gptq or --quantization awq. The serving framework handles kernel selection. The engineer sets the flag, versions the artifact with a tag like llama3-8b-gptq-int4-g128 (g128 = group size 128, a quantization parameter that trades quality against memory overhead), and deploys.

Think of choosing a compression level in nginx: gzip_comp_level 6 vs. 9. The server doesn’t know about the original content — it just applies compression at serve time. --quantization gptq is the same: the inference server applies the kernel at load time, the model is pre-quantized, the flag says which kernel to use. The engineer sets the flag; the framework handles everything else.

Quantization is a cost lever that lives in the deployment config. Learn to pull it, and you own a 2–5x cost reduction on GPU spend without changing a line of model code.

Bridge to Day 18 code

Today’s ebpf-llm-tracer work adds a WAL and rate limiter to the Go consumer. There’s a direct parallel: the WAL decides which events are too important to drop, and the rate limiter decides which new events to shed under pressure.

In the quantization world, the calibration dataset plays the WAL role — it decides which weights are too important to aggressively quantize. The decision matrix is the rate limiter: under GPU memory pressure, you shed precision from less important weights first and preserve it on the salient ones. AWQ names the salient weights explicitly. GPTQ discovers them via Hessian approximation. bitsandbytes doesn’t differentiate at all — which is exactly the trade you make when you choose iteration speed over production quality.

The shared instinct across both: know what you can afford to lose before pressure hits, not during it. The calibration step and the bucket capacity are both exercises in deciding the loss tolerance in advance, so the system can handle degraded conditions without making ad-hoc decisions under load.
